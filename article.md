# LLM QB Agent — What We Had to Add (and Why)

Notes for article. Tracks every scaffold addition made to get the model to behave like a real QB.

---

## Base Setup

- QB is an LLM (gpt-4o-mini / gpt-5-nano) called every 0.1s timestep
- Receives: current positions, speeds, headings of WR and CB, current separation, movement history
- Outputs: `hold` or `throw` with target coordinates and ball speed in mph
- WR runs a scripted route. CB plays scripted man coverage.
- No minimum hold time — QB was free to throw from snap

---

## Additions

**Hardcoded minimum hold time (0.5s)**
The QB was calling throw at t=0.0 before the WR had moved a single step — there was no history to reason from. Added a hard 0.5s gate before the LLM is even consulted. Without this, the model had no signal at all and just threw immediately.

**Route schedule in observation (cut time + heading + estimated cut position)**
The QB had no idea when or where the WR would cut. It was hallucinating target coordinates wildly — throwing to (36, 90) on a slant where the WR starts at x=16 and never gets past x=24. Added a per-timestep route schedule showing each cut's time, direction, and estimated WR position at cut time based on current speed/heading. Grounded the model's spatial reasoning significantly.

**Heading labels (plain English directions)**
The model was treating heading angles as raw numbers with no feel for field geometry. "Cut to 40°" meant nothing — it would interpret it as a huge rightward move. Added plain English labels: "diagonal upfield-right (toward right sideline)", "straight back toward QB", etc. alongside every angle.

**Removed projected throw options (pre-computed separation at arrival)**
The original observation pre-computed WR and CB positions for 5 time horizons and showed separation at arrival. This was meant to help but it backfired — the model saw "3.2 yd separation, feasible" and just threw without reasoning about whether the route had actually developed. Removing it forced the model to do its own reasoning from raw history.

**Ball travel time reference (bullet / regular / lob)**
The model had no concept of how long a throw takes to arrive. It would target a spot 1.3 seconds away and throw a "bullet" that arrived in 0.27s — completely mistimed. Added a per-timestep line showing actual flight time to the WR's current position at three speed tiers (bullet = max mph, regular = mid, lob = min). The model could now at least understand the magnitude of travel time.

**Two-pass decision system**
Single-shot decisions let the model commit to a throw without checking whether the WR would actually be at the landing spot. Added pass 1 (read the field — where do you think you want to throw?) and pass 2 (here are the concrete options at that target — will the WR actually be there?). Separates intent from commitment.

**WR projected position at arrival shown in pass 2 options**
Even with two passes, the model was targeting a future WR waypoint (e.g. "where WR will be at cut time") and treating it as a valid throw target right now. In pass 2, added a column showing where the WR will actually be at ball arrival for each speed option, plus whether it's catchable or a miss. The model now sees "MISS by 11.7yd" for every option when the route hasn't developed yet, and holds correctly.

**CB lockup duration (2 seconds before reaction delay kicks in)**
In replays the CB was giving up 4+ yards of separation within the first second. The CB was chasing a delayed WR signal from frame 0, so as the WR accelerated upfield the CB fell behind immediately. Added a 2-second lockup phase where the CB mirrors the WR's current position directly before the reaction delay engages. Now the CB stays tight for a realistic duration before the cut creates separation.

**PBU requires proximity (1 yard) or CB in passing lane**
A PBU was firing at 5.91 yards of separation — completely unrealistic. A cornerback 6 yards away cannot break up a pass. Changed resolution logic so a PBU is only possible if the CB is within 1 yard of the WR at arrival, or is physically in the passing lane (within 1.5 yards of the line between the landing spot and the WR).

**Pending-cut problem: pass 2 projections are wrong before the cut fires**
The two-pass system showed "MISS by 11yd" for every option while the route was developing — correct behavior. But the model would sometimes commit anyway at t=1.6s on a slant where the cut fires at t=2.0s. The pass 2 options use current WR heading/speed to project position at arrival. Pre-cut, those projections point the WR running straight upfield forever — so the landing spot is never near the projected WR. The model sees "MISS" and should hold, but occasionally reasons "the WR will be near there *after* the cut" and commits early. The fix is to make the pending-cut warning stronger in the pass 2 prompt: if a cut is imminent, the projections are explicitly labeled unreliable.

**CB agent: the model chose the wrong backpedal direction**
First run of the CB agent, the CB sprinted 22 yards the wrong way. The observation told the model "backpedaling means heading ~180°" and included an example — so when the CB was already *upfield* of the WR (between WR and end zone, which is the correct position), it read "backpedal = 180°" and ran *downfield through the WR* and kept going toward the QB. The CB went from 5 yd separation to 22 yd separation in 2.3 seconds. The fix wasn't physics — it was observation scaffolding. We replaced the abstract heading-vs-facing explanation with a **SITUATION block** that pre-computes the specific situation and emits a RECOMMENDED action with exact numbers: "WR is 4.2 yd downfield of you — backpedal upfield, heading ≈ 0°, facing ≈ 180°." The model follows prescriptions much more reliably than deriving the correct heading from a description of the coordinate system.

**CB agent: the model kept backpedaling after the WR blew past it**
Even after fixing direction, the CB would correctly backpedal while the WR approached, but then continue backpedaling *away* once the WR passed it. The situation flipped from "WR approaching" to "WR running away" but the model just kept doing the same thing. The SITUATION block needed to encode the phase transition explicitly: three named states — "WR approaching (backpedal)", "WR just passed (close gap)", "WR beaten by 2+ yd (CHASE)" — each with its own RECOMMENDED action and unambiguous instruction to flip hips and sprint. Without naming the phase change, the model doesn't detect it.

**ScriptedQB lead throw: naive projection vs. route simulation**
The first implementation of `ScriptedQB` used linear projection: take WR's current heading and speed, multiply by flight time, throw there. This broke badly the moment the WR was mid-cut — the WR's heading had just flipped to 40° and speed had shed from the cut, so the projection pointed way off field. The correct approach is obvious in retrospect: the WR's route is *fully deterministic* — we have `ScriptedWR.move()` and know the exact physics. The fix was to step the WR forward along its route using the same `move()` call the game loop uses, for exactly `eta` seconds. This is always accurate regardless of route phase. The lesson: when you have a scripted/deterministic system, simulate it — don't approximate it.

**Fuzzy landing zone: modeling human vision**
When the ball is in the air, a real DB doesn't know the exact landing spot immediately — they read trajectory and narrow in. We modeled this as ±4 yd uncertainty at release, tightening linearly to ±0.25 yd at arrival. The CB observation shows this shrinking zone each step. In practice the model latches onto the zone center from the first step anyway (treats it as point knowledge), so the fuzz is currently cosmetic. But it sets up future behavior where the CB must commit a direction before knowing the exact spot — which is the interesting decision.

**CB comeback route: the SITUATION block had a missing state**
The CB observation pre-computes a SITUATION block with a RECOMMENDED action. Originally three states, all keyed on `dy` (who is upfield of whom). On a slant or go route this works fine: CB backpedals while WR approaches, flips hips when WR passes. On a comeback, the WR runs upfield past the CB, then reverses to 180° and runs back toward the QB. After the cut, `dy` goes negative — CB is upfield of WR — and the old code emitted "you are between WR and end zone ✓ → backpedal." The CB dutifully backpedaled further upfield while the WR ran away in the opposite direction. Separation hit 14.17 yd at the catch. The fix was a fourth state: check `wr.heading` — if it's in 135–225° (running toward QB) AND CB is upfield, emit "WR has cut BACK toward QB — flip hips and CHASE downfield. Do NOT backpedal." After the fix: 0.1 yd separation, DROP. The lesson: the SITUATION block encodes field geometry logic that the LLM should not be trusted to derive. Every route type that can flip the positional relationship needs its own named state.

**Stateless calls mean the LLM can't detect phase transitions across steps**
Each timestep is a fresh two-message LLM call. The model has no memory of its previous reasoning or what it decided last step. It only sees the current observation plus a WR history table (last 10 steps of positions/headings embedded as text). This means phase transitions — "WR just cut, switch from backpedal to chase" — must be detected and expressed in the observation scaffolding, not left to the model to infer from the history. The history table contains the signal (heading changes), but the model won't reliably act on it without the SITUATION block calling it out explicitly with a named state and a RECOMMENDED action. Scaffolding is doing the perception work; the LLM is just executing the named response.

**Intent-aware facing during ball-in-air movement**
Once the ball is in the air the CB locks an intent (swat, go_for_pick, play_man). Early versions told the CB to always face the landing zone when the ball was in the air — correct for swat/INT, but wrong for play_man, where the CB should face the receiver (no facing requirement for play_man in resolution). The fix was to pass the locked intent into the movement observation and emit a FACING line that varies by intent: swat/INT → face the zone, play_man → face the WR and head toward the zone. This also captures the real football logic: a CB playing the receiver on a comeback should be in the receiver's face at the catch, not turning to look at the ball.

**WR agent: the model cut to the route heading immediately (1.8s early)**
The system prompt said "the cut time is a strict guideline (±0.2s)" but the live prompt said "if CB is giving cushion, consider cutting early to exploit it." The model resolved the contradiction the greedy way: it jumped to heading 40° at t=0.2 on a slant with cut_time=2.0. The route was telegraphed 1.8 seconds early. Fix: remove the early-cut escape hatch entirely, and replace it with phase-explicit instructions. The observation now shows which phase the WR is in with a hard directive ("KEEP HEADING NEAR 0°. Do NOT move to 40° yet"). Contradictions in the prompt are resolved by the model, not the prompt — always in the direction of least resistance.

**WR deception: "jab step" meant nothing without a mechanical definition**
The initial system prompt described deception as "false steps, speed changes, body fakes." The model's output: reasonable-sounding reasoning ("jabbing inside before the slant cut") with heading=40° every single step. It understood the concept but had no way to express it — there are no animations in a 2D simulation. A jab step IS a heading change. Fixing this required explaining exactly how the CB tracks the WR: it reads heading and speed, projects forward 0.5s, and moves to that point. Once the model understood this, it immediately started outputting heading=330° for one step then snapping back to 0° — correct behavior. The lesson: tell the model what the CB *computes*, not what football moves look like.

**WR deception: model understood "jab" but executed it for 20 steps**
After adding the mechanical definition, the model correctly jabbed left (330°) — but held that heading for the entire pre-cut phase (2 seconds). A jab that lasts 2 seconds is just running the wrong direction. The word "jab" implies brevity; the model interpreted it as a technique to apply, not a 1-step move to execute and release. Fix: show the exact step-by-step pattern explicitly ("step N: 330°, step N+1: 0°, step N+2: 0°, step N+3: jab again"). Also show the *wrong* pattern ("330°, 330°, 330° — this is NOT a jab, this is running left"). Making the failure mode explicit was as important as making the correct pattern explicit.

**call_t race condition: state mutation order matters**
The WR `decide()` call returned `call_for_ball: true` and the runner set `wr_agent.call_t = t` after the call returned. The *next* step's observation then read `wr_agent.call_t` to format "COMMITTED: called at t=X.Xs" — but `call_t` was still None because `decide()` hadn't been called yet for the new step. Fix: move `self.call_t = t` inside `decide()` at the moment of call validation, before returning. Observation reads state that was set during the *previous* decide() call — the runner setting state after the fact is always one step behind.

**CB "freedom" was fake: prescriptive scaffolding overrode the model**
The CB agent prompt claimed to give the CB free decision-making, but the SITUATION block was emitting `RECOMMENDED: backpedal, heading=0°, facing=180°` and `cb_pass1.txt` said "Copy the heading and facing numbers EXACTLY from the RECOMMENDED line. Do not compute your own." The CB was a lookup table with LLM overhead — it was never making a decision. The deeper problem: `_cb_situation()` had a geometry gap. WR jabs at 330° and 30° fall outside the `wr_going_lateral` threshold (45–135° or 225–315°), so they never triggered the lateral/intercept path — they always fell into the `elif dy < -0.5: backpedal` branch. Even if the CB had been free to decide, it was getting incorrect geometry analysis. The lesson: freedom in the prompt text is meaningless if the observation scaffolding has already pre-selected the answer.

**WR deception had no feedback loop**
The WR's history table previously showed only its own headings and speeds — no CB data except the static current-step snapshot. The WR could jab left all day with no way to know whether the CB was reacting at all. Deception without feedback is just randomness. Adding a side-by-side MOVE LOG (WR hdg/spd + CB hdg/mode/Δhdg per step) gives the WR the observability it needs: did my jab at t=1.2 actually change what the CB did at t=1.3? A Δhdg of 0° means the jab was invisible; a Δhdg of 15°+ means the CB reacted. The model can now close the loop.

## Observations

- The model's biggest failure mode is spatial: it has no grounded sense of where players are relative to each other or how fast they move across the field. Every scaffold addition was essentially providing that grounding.
- Pre-computing things "to help" often backfired (projected separation, min hold time from scenario YAML) because the model would anchor on the pre-computed number instead of reading the actual situation.
- The two-pass system revealed that the model's intent and its commitment were disconnected — it would say "I want to throw here" and then pick an option that physically couldn't reach the receiver.
- Travel time was a consistent blind spot. The model understood the concept when told, but couldn't apply it correctly without seeing explicit "WR will be at X when ball arrives" numbers.
- The two-pass system partially solved premature throws but introduced a subtler failure: the model reasons about *future* WR state (post-cut) while the projections show *current* heading extrapolated forward. It's doing the right qualitative reasoning ("WR will be open after cut") but committing before the cut validates that. The scaffold showed MISS correctly; the model overrode it with narrative reasoning.
- Tooling footgun (Ollama): `response_format={"type": "json_object"}` causes qwen3:8b via Ollama's OpenAI-compat API to return empty `content`. The thinking goes to `model_extra["reasoning"]` and the answer never makes it to `content`. Removing the format constraint and falling back to the reasoning field fixes it — but the symptom (silent empty string) is a bad failure mode that looked like a hang.
- qwen3:8b without `json_object` constraint: 73% parse error rate (16/22 calls) — model outputs prose essays in `content` instead of JSON. The rare successful calls were correct (held until t=2.2s post-cut, CATCH outcome), but the model isn't reliable enough for local use without a better JSON-forcing mechanism. Prompt engineering or a smaller/faster instruct model would help more than further API tweaks.

**Cut detection: a threshold gate isn't enough when the LLM jabs at the threshold**
The first cut-detection fix raised the gate to `t >= 0.5` to skip early jabs. In the next run, the WR jabbed at exactly t=0.5 — every route, every run. The model hadn't "learned" to time its jab to defeat the gate; it just always jabs early, and 0.5s is inside the early-jab window. A fixed time gate becomes fragile the moment the model has a consistent habit. The real fix is structural: require heading persistence (2+ consecutive steps) with a snap-back test. A jab by definition returns to 0° within one step; a real cut holds. The detector now uses a two-step candidate system — a heading change creates a candidate, which is either confirmed (held) or discarded (snapped back) on the next step.

**Richer deception coaching did not break the template habit**
After adding detailed deception coaching — hold fakes, speed fakes, double fakes, explicit anti-repetition rules — the WR still jabbed 330°/30° on every single route across all 10 runs. The habit appears to be at training-data depth, not context depth. Prompt instructions to "vary your fakes" or "don't use the same angle twice" are not strong enough to override a deeply baked pattern. What hasn't been tried: showing the WR its own action history so it can observe the repetition in context, or an explicit per-step callout ("you have jabbed 330° four times this play — the CB has read it"). The second is likely stronger because it makes the failure concrete and present rather than abstract and future.

**When the LLM misreads a heading, the error is systematic**
On the comeback route, the QB consistently threw upfield when the WR was running downfield (heading 180°). The LEAD HINT used angle notation that the model conflated with "running far upfield." The model doesn't compute in coordinate space; it maps angles to football concepts. Heading=180° plus a projected y higher than current produced a throw upfield — the model reasoned "comeback" ≈ "throw short to the sideline" without processing that y=63.7 is upfield of the WR's actual position at y=62.4. The fix was explicit English: "y is DECREASING toward QB." This bypasses the model's angle-to-direction inference entirely. Whenever an angle is semantically ambiguous in football terms (180° means "downfield" in coordinate space but "back toward QB" in football vocabulary), add a prose disambiguation — don't trust the model to derive it.

**The constraint "we guide, not enforce" determines which problems are fixable**
Several Round 2 problems (WR exits fake phase early, QB throws a lob on a short horizontal route, WR drifts sideways during ball-in-air) could each be fixed with a code-level rule. But the experiment is designed to measure LLM spatial decision-making. If we add a floor on fake phase duration, a speed requirement for horizontal routes, or a heading lock during ball-in-air, we're no longer measuring the LLM — we're measuring our own rules. These problems are left open as data: the model made a bad football decision, and that's what we're measuring. This constraint filters every observation and prompt change: the question is always "does this give the agent better information?" not "does this guarantee a better outcome?"

**WR calls for ball before executing the cut — the most common failure across all short-cut routes**
After four runs, the dominant single failure mode is the WR calling `call_for_ball=true` while still running straight upfield (heading=0°), well before the route cut should execute. On slant (cut at t≈2.0s, cut heading ≈315°), corner (cut at t≈2.5s, cut heading ≈290°), in (cut at t≈1.8s, cut heading ≈270°), and curl (cut at t≈1.8s, cut heading ≈180°), the WR called at t=0.5–1.0 while still at heading=0°. Each one resulted in a PBU, DROP, or INT — the QB threw immediately to where the WR was going (upfield), not where the route called for. The WR is applying one decision criterion ("I'm open by 2+ yards") as a sufficient condition for calling, when it's actually necessary-but-not-sufficient. The real condition is "I'm open AND my heading is approximately correct for the catch." The WR knows the catch heading — it's in the observation — but isn't applying it as a gating condition on the call. This is a case where the model's helpfulness instinct works against it: seeing separation, it wants to close the loop and call for the ball, without checking whether it's in the right position to catch it.

**Outcome variance across identical seeds: same route, different failure mode across runs**
The corner route gives a clear example of variance across runs without any code changes. Round 2: CATCH (WR executed the 290° break, reached 1.73 yd separation, abandoned it — O2). Round 3: DROP (WR abandoned break, regained separation via speed, called late). Round 4: PBU (WR called at t=0.5 heading=0°, never ran the corner cut at all). Three different failure modes from the same model, same seed, same scenario. The model's stochasticity produces a different "mistake archetype" each run. This means a single run is not a reliable diagnostic — a problem that appears fixed (O2 didn't manifest in Run 4) may just be hidden behind a different failure. Multiple runs are needed to distinguish "fixed" from "masked."

**MOVE LOG gave the WR visibility into CB reactions — the CB still didn't react**
The side-by-side MOVE LOG was added so the WR could see whether its jabs were moving the CB. The ΔHdg column shows how much the CB's heading changed after each WR move. In every route across every run, ΔHdg ≈ 0° on jabs. The CB simply isn't reacting to the WR's 330°/30° jabs at all — it stays in backpedal at heading 0° regardless. So the MOVE LOG is showing the WR exactly what's happening: your fakes aren't working. The WR's response: continue the same fakes. This suggests the failure is not observability (the WR can now see the feedback) but response to negative feedback — the model knows its fakes aren't working but doesn't switch strategies. The proposed fix (explicit per-step prohibition: "do NOT use 330° again this play") bypasses the need for the model to reason from failure toward strategy change, and instead makes the constraint structural.

**The call_tolerance gate existed in comments but never in code**
Four runs into A4, P-NEW emerged as the dominant failure: WR calling `call_for_ball=true` while still running 0° upfield, 1–1.5 seconds before the route cut. The state.md listed "call_tolerance gate in runner.py (±45° check vs cut_heading)" as a known mechanism. Auditing runner.py revealed it never existed — the comment described intended behavior that was never implemented. The call fired immediately whenever the LLM returned `call_for_ball: true`, with no heading validation at all. The lesson: a described mechanism is not an implemented mechanism. When a bug persists despite "existing" scaffolding, audit whether the scaffolding is actually in the code.

The obvious fix — add a 5-line heading gate that suppresses calls before the cut — was written and then reverted. The experiment constraint is that agents are guided via prompt information, never mechanically forced. Suppressing the call in code means the WR can never make the mistake, which means we can never measure whether the prompt guidance actually works. The approach instead: tell the WR in the prompt why calling early is self-defeating ("calling upfield means the QB throws upfield, not to your break point") and ask it to anticipate its future position along the route before deciding to call. If this fails, we learn something real about prompt limits. If a gate had been left in, we'd only learn that the gate worked.

**The WR applies "am I open now?" as a sufficient condition for calling — but the right condition is "will I be open at the catch?"**
The WR's pre-cut calls all share the same structure: current separation >2 yd, therefore call. The route cut is the future event that creates the catching geometry — but the WR is optimizing for present separation, not future catch position. At t=0.8 on a slant, a WR who is 2 yd from the CB while running straight upfield will NOT be 2 yd from the CB when the ball arrives at the slant catch point; the CB is running a closing trajectory. This is the anticipation failure your notes described: "the WR is basing everything on its separation right now. It is assuming the CB will keep this separation the whole time." The hard gate (heading must be within 45° of cut_heading) enforces the structural precondition, but the prompt change — explicitly asking the WR to project its own future position along the route and the CB's future position from its current trajectory — is the attempt to install the actual football reasoning. Whether the model applies this anticipation correctly without the hard gate is the thing to watch in Run 5.

**The persistent scratchpad: giving the WR memory across steps**
Each WR step is a stateless LLM call with no memory of prior reasoning. The WR was re-deciding to "fake left for 2-4 steps" every single step — it had been faking left for 3.7 seconds straight and still thought it was on step 1. The fix was a `wr_note` field in the JSON response: one line, ~120 chars, that the agent writes to itself and reads back next step via the observation. The key constraints: (1) overwrite it every step — it's a plan, not a log; (2) verify it against the move log — the note is not ground truth; (3) think forward, not just about right now. In practice, the WR still hallucinated ("2-step fake completed" after 1 step), but less severely. The mechanism is right; the grounding is incomplete. The next logical step is injecting the actual step count per heading from the simulation side ("you have been on 270° for N steps") so the WR can't miscount.

**The facing instruction/scoring mismatch: a bug hiding as a model failure**
The WR kept facing ~213° during ball-in-air when it should have been ~180°. The observation said "Bearing to landing zone: 36° — use this for FACING." But resolution.py scores facing based on angle to the *QB*, not the landing zone. On a slant, the landing zone is upfield of the WR (bearing ~36°) while the QB is behind (bearing ~185°). These are nearly opposite directions. The observation was telling the WR to do the wrong thing, and when it did the right thing (facing toward QB), the observation label called it wrong. The fix was to show "QB is at bearing X° from you — the ball is coming from that direction" and explain the concept rather than prescribe the angle. The lesson: when facing behavior looks like a model reasoning failure, check whether the observation itself is giving contradictory or wrong information first.

**Freedom philosophy: context replaces instructions**
The WR agent started with a highly prescriptive system — phase-by-phase directives ("CURRENT: stem phase, run 0°, HOLD this heading for the FULL phase duration"), pre-computed jab angle blacklists, turn cost tables. The redesign stripped all of this and replaced it with context: where the CB is, what the CB has been doing, what the CB's tracking mechanic computes, and how deception actually works mechanically. The agent is told the *physics* (CB projects WR forward 0.5s along current heading), not the *procedure* (jab left then go right). The philosophy: failures are data, not bugs. If the WR fakes wrong, that's a measured LLM decision under real context, not a missing rule. The tension: some failures (hallucinating step count, facing wrong direction) are not really "decisions" — they're information gaps the model can't reason through without scaffolding. The line between "give it context" and "give it a rule" isn't always obvious; the test is whether the scaffolding encodes geometry the model can't derive, or behavior we've decided it should have.

**Route description position in the observation matters: first is loudest**
The WR observation had a route description block — specific phase headings and timings — but it appeared after the header, pre-snap plan, note, WR position, CB position, and play state. In practice, the WR was reading its own burst status before knowing what route it was running. The fix was to move the route description to line 1 of the observation body, before anything else. The principle: LLMs weight earlier context more heavily. If the most important instruction is "you are running a slant, here is exactly what you need to do," it should not be buried after four paragraphs of positional data.

**"Max separation" was the wrong success criterion — it tells the WR to keep running**
The call-for-ball prompt originally told the WR to call when it expected "max separation." The problem: max implies keep going — maximize, don't stop until it's the highest it will ever be. In practice the WR would never call because there was always the possibility of more separation if it ran further. The right criterion is "enough separation" — body gap >= 1.5 yards is a high-probability catch. Changing the language to "signal when you expect enough separation (body gap >= 1.5 yd) in the next 0.1-0.3 seconds" also shifts the mental model from a continuous optimization to a threshold check. One word change; different decision logic implied.

**Route geometry is determined by yardage, not just heading angle**
The initial route timings were set by feel, and several were badly wrong. A "slant" with a 1.0s stem (~6 yards) is a very different route from a slant with a 2.0s stem (~14 yards) — the first is a short crossing route, the second is a proper slant at NFL depth. When the route description only showed "Head 0° for ~1.0s" without a yardage figure, the WR had no spatial grounding for what the route looked like. Adding physics-accurate yardage ("Head 0° for ~14 yards (~2.0s)") gives the WR a field-position anchor — it can compare "I started at y=0, I am now at y=7, I still need to travel ~7 more yards before cutting." Yardage connects the timing to the field; time alone doesn't.

**Removing option labels unlocked genuine CB spatial reasoning — one change, immediate result**
The CB was picking "backpedal" every step because `_cb_situation()` emitted three named options ("backpedal / intercept / mirror") with pre-written tradeoffs, and `cb_pass1.txt`'s example JSON showed `"mode": "backpedal", "reasoning": "WR is still approaching and I want to keep cushion"`. The LLM anchored on the example and the first option. Across 10 routes and 5 runs, the CB's heading was 0°–10° on almost every stem-phase step. The fix was to remove all option labels and tradeoffs from `_cb_situation()` entirely — replace with a lean `GEOMETRY:` block (separation, bearing, WR projected position in 0.5s, intercept bearing) — and swap the example JSON to a forward-close movement. First run after the change: on slant_42, the CB varied its heading continuously (190°, 139°, 60°, 69°, 355°, 59°, 36°, 35°, 38°, 39°...), ran laterally at 36°–39° for five consecutive steps matching the WR's 40° break, and made a legitimate PBU at 1.24 yd separation. Prior run: pure backpedal every step, separation=4.83 yd, DROP. The mechanism: named options are prompts to pattern-match against, not prompts to reason from. "Backpedal" plus an example that shows backpedal means the model fills in backpedal. Raw geometry with no option names forces the model to actually read the data and decide.

**The QB's "wait for the WR call" rule was secretly the only timing logic it had**
The QB had no independence: it threw when (and only when) the WR called for the ball. To give it autonomy — throw before the call if it sees the window opening, hold after the call if coverage is tight — we removed the "do not throw until the WR signals" lines from the prompt and observation. The QB immediately threw at t=0.5s on every route, straight into coverage. The lesson: "wait for the call" wasn't a passivity bug, it was *load-bearing*. It was the only thing encoding route timing — the crude proxy that kept the QB from throwing before the route developed. Stripping it revealed the QB had no independent model of *when* a window exists; freedom without the situational understanding to use it just produces instant bad throws. Autonomy isn't subtractive (remove the gate); it's additive (give the agent the judgment the gate was faking).

**"Open" is a prediction, not an observation — and the system was making the prediction for the QB**
The first autonomy attempt had the observation *declare* state: "CUT CONFIRMED — you may throw now," "DO NOT throw." That's the same prescriptive scaffolding that turned the CB into a lookup table, just aimed at the QB. The reframe: teach the QB that a throw is a *forecast*. "Open" isn't a current state you wait to observe — it's a prediction that a throwable gap will exist at the catch point, built from two layers: the route design gives the *expectation* (the prior — where the WR is trying to go), and the real position/heading/velocity history of both players *confirms or corrects* it. The system now reports only facts (has the WR's heading swung onto the route's break yet?) and hands the openness judgment back to the QB. Net effect on curl: from chronic INCOMPLETE to CATCH, with the QB throwing on its own read at t=2.7s rather than mechanically on the WR's call.

**Break-gated logic silently fails on routes with no break**
The "has the WR's cut happened yet?" direction check gated on `expected_open_t < 9.0` and the route having a defining break heading. A go route has neither — its `route_phases` is `[(999, 0.0)]` (straight upfield forever) and it has no `expected_open_t`. So the go route fell through every branch into the default "direction not confirmed — wait for the break," and the QB waited for a cut that never comes. It took a SACK holding the ball with **6.62 yards** of separation downfield. The fix was a `straight_route` branch: if every route phase heads upfield, the WR's direction is confirmed from the snap and openness is purely a footrace (has he overtaken the CB with separation that holds). The general lesson: any logic phrased as "wait until event X happens" needs an explicit answer for the inputs where X *never* happens, or it deadlocks — the absence of a break is itself a confirmed direction, not an unconfirmed one.

**The lead hint must project off the locked call heading, not the live physical heading**
On a curl, the WR plants and the cut takes a step or two; at the exact moment it calls for the ball, its *instantaneous* heading can read 270° (a transient sideways plant step) even though its *locked* call heading — the direction it's actually committed to — is 180° back toward the QB. The QB's LEAD HINT was extrapolating off the live 270° reading and throwing 5+ yards to the side of a receiver who was hooking straight back. The fix: once the WR has called, the heading is mechanically locked, so project the lead off `wr_call_heading`, not the noisy physical heading. A single transient frame at a plant is never a reliable basis for extrapolation — trust the committed/locked value.

**Static acceleration made the entire deception system fictional**
The physics engine had a single flat acceleration constant applied every step regardless of speed. A player at rest and a player at 7 yd/s both gained `accel * dt = 1.2 yd/s` per step. This meant: (1) there was no burst advantage from executing a plant-and-go correctly, (2) a WR who got their hips turned the wrong way could recover instantly, and (3) the CB could close at the same rate whether it had just committed to a hard cut or not. All the deception logic in the prompts — fakes, hip commits, stutter steps — was coaching agents on a mechanic that physically did not exist. You could write the best possible deception prompt and it would produce identical outcomes to a random heading, because the physics didn't differentiate them.

The fix: dynamic burst (`accel * (1 - speed/max_speed)`) plus a `cut_recovery` step counter. Any hard cut >35° at speed >3 yd/s triggers N steps of reduced acceleration — the player's hips are committed to the old direction and their weight is still shifting. A 90° cut at full speed costs ~4 steps of ~25% burst capacity. This is the mechanic that actually makes a hip-commit fake valuable: the WR plans the cut, so they have no recovery. The CB reacts to it, so they pay the penalty. The window is real now.

**The right level of abstraction for physics is "consequences", not "numbers"**
After implementing cut_recovery, the decision was how to surface it. Option A: show the raw number (`cut_recovery=3`). Option B: translate it into consequences (`RECOVERING — 3 steps left, burst ~43%`). Option C: an explicit alert when the opponent is in recovery (`"!! CB HIP-TURNED — 3 steps left. THIS IS YOUR WINDOW"`). All three are in the observation now, at different levels. The key insight is that agents will not derive consequences from numbers reliably — "cut_recovery=3" means nothing to a model that doesn't have a grounded sense of how 3 steps at reduced acceleration translates to yards of separation gained. The explicit alert ("THIS IS YOUR WINDOW") bypasses all that inference and gives the model a named decision point. Same information; the only question is whether the model reads it correctly or acts on it correctly. Named alerts are acted on; numbers are noted.

**The scratchpad feedback loop: hallucinated history becomes injected context**
The WR has a one-line `wr_note` scratchpad — it writes a note to itself each step, and the next step's observation injects it as "YOUR NOTE (from last step)." At t=0.0, the move log is empty (no actions have occurred) and the note field reads "(none yet)." Despite this, the LLM outputs `wr_note = "jabbed left last step so returning to 0 now"` on the very first step. That hallucinated note is then injected into t=0.1 as fact. The LLM reads its own invented history, confirms it, and the loop runs for the entire play — the boilerplate string appeared verbatim on 6 of 10 routes, including mid-fake on the zig (where it aborted a real 3-step fake at t=0.9 by claiming to be "returning to 0"). The fix is a grounding rule: the note must be consistent with what the move log shows, and if the log is empty, the note must be forward-looking. But the deeper issue is that anything injected into the prompt as "what you did" will be treated as fact by the model, even if the model wrote it itself one step ago as a confabulation. Scratchpad memory and ground truth are not the same thing, and the observation needs to make that distinction explicit every step.

**The CB backpedal problem was ideological, not mechanical**
After the physics overhaul, the CB observation included burst status, cut_recovery columns in history, explicit "WR HIP-TURNED — CLOSE NOW" alerts, and raw bearing geometry. The CB still spent 15–25 consecutive steps per route with near-identical reasoning ("WR is still approaching, keeping cushion"), including steps after the WR had already completed its cut and was running away. The issue wasn't missing data — the CB had everything it needed. The issue was the ideology: `cb_system.txt` said "patience beats reactivity" and "do not match heading changes immediately," and provided no failure anchor. The CB had no model of what losing looked like. It didn't know that backpedaling straight upfield while the WR drifts sideways is already a loss, or that continuing to backpedal after a confirmed cut means every step costs a yard it can't recover. Without a concrete danger model ("3+ yd separation on break heading + ball in air = catch"), the patience doctrine applied to everything — even situations where immediate reaction was obviously required. Correct behavior and safety behavior coincided on most steps, which reinforced the template. The CB only escaped it when a ball-in-air intent switch fired.

**A 3-step code gate was categorized as a prompt/behavioral problem**
Round 7's problems.md listed "S3 — CB frozen for 3 steps at pre-snap (HIGH, ALL ROUTES)" and the hypothesis was "make the physics prompting more prominent." The root cause was a single line in runner.py: `if cb_agent is not None and "CB1" in states and t >= 0.3:`. The CB agent literally wasn't called for t=0.0, 0.1, 0.2 — it wasn't making bad decisions, it wasn't being called at all. The free WR release wasn't a reasoning failure; it was a hardcoded gate. When a behavioral symptom appears consistently across all routes and all runs, check for code-level causes before assuming prompt causes. A behavior that is perfectly consistent across all runs is more likely structural than stochastic.

**_build_options assumed constant WR speed, making post-cut throws systematically wrong**
The QB's pass-2 option table showed "WR will be at (X, Y) when ball arrives" for each throw speed, using `wr_speed * eta` to project position. This assumed the WR's current speed would hold for the entire flight. On post-cut routes, the WR is in cut_recovery at call time — speed is 2–6 yd/s and rebuilding. The QB would throw to (20.3, 63.5) because the projection showed the WR there, but the WR would accelerate through that spot and be at 64.3 by arrival. The QB was computing the right lead for a WR running at constant 2 yd/s; the WR was running at 7 yd/s by the time the ball got there. The fix adds a two-row projection per option when cut_recovery > 0: one using current speed (conservative, where the WR will be if recovery is slow) and one using rebuilt max speed (optimistic, where the WR will be if they accelerate normally). This gives the QB a range and flags the uncertainty, rather than presenting a single incorrect projection as fact.

**cut_recovery was working in physics but absent from every replay**
The physics engine correctly computed cut_recovery after the overhaul — speed drops were clean and proportional to cut angle. But the `_player_snap()` function that builds each step's replay JSON simply never included `cut_recovery` in the output dict. Agents were referencing "rec" values in their reasoning, but those values couldn't be verified from replays because the field wasn't there. The physics were real; the observability wasn't. This is a different failure mode from agents ignoring available data — the data existed in the running simulation but never reached the persistent record. When validating a new physics feature, check that the feature's state is actually written to replay output, not just that the physics code runs correctly.

**Switching models resets all behavioral progress — new failure modes replace old ones**
Moving from gpt-5-nano to qwen3:8b via Ollama produced an entirely different failure profile. The boilerplate "jabbed left last step" hallucination (which ran for 7 rounds with GPT) disappeared — replaced by route shape confusion: the WR executed a generic fake-then-break template on 6 of 10 routes, ignoring the route geometry entirely. "Go" became a fake-cut near the line of scrimmage. "Slant" became a seam. "Curl" became a 9-step lateral drift. The model had learned none of the route-specific context from prior prompt work — it needed to re-learn it with its own training distribution. Behavioral calibration is model-specific, not prompt-specific.

**The model generated the old API schema even after the schema changed**
The QB pass-2 prompt specifies `{"action": "throw", "option": "<label>", "reasoning": "..."}` — the model picks a speed tier by label (bullet/hard/medium/soft/lob) and the runner resolves coordinates from the label. Three consecutive throw attempts in one run produced parse errors with this raw: `{"action":"throw","target_coord":[17.5,53.5],"ball_speed_mph":45,"reasoning":"Lead WR...`. The model was generating the *previous* schema — direct coordinates and speed — instead of the current label format. `response_format={"type":"json_object"}` guarantees valid JSON, not correct field names. The model's training distribution included the old format; the current prompt description wasn't strong enough to override it. Fix: add a fallback parser that accepts both formats. The diagnostic was only possible because we added full raw logging — the original 120-char truncation showed a syntactically incomplete string that looked like a different error entirely.

**Call timing is asymmetric: the window closes before it looks open**
The WR consistently called for the ball 1–2 steps after CB recovery had started, not at the start. At t=2.7, after observing "CB rec=3 and 2 yd gap exists", the WR wrote: "increase speed to force separation before CB recovers" — then called at t=2.8. With CB 3-step recovery starting at t=2.6, the throw at t=2.9 arrived at t=3.2 after the window had fully closed. The model knew it had a window but treated it as time to gain *more* separation, not as the throw window itself. The fix that worked: a "CALL TIMING" note explaining the math explicitly ("QB takes 1 step to process, ball takes 0.2-0.5s to fly — call at rec=3, ball arrives at rec≈0; call at rec=1, ball arrives after recovery"). Also critical: "CB rec > 0 IS clear separation — you do not need to see the gap grow visually first." Without that second sentence, the model acknowledged the window and waited for visual confirmation that it had grown. By then it was gone.

**Separation in real football is 1.5 yards, not 3**
The original system used 3.0 yd center-to-center as "open." Real NFL coverage is considered broken at ~1.5 yd edge-to-edge air gap between bodies. With 0.5 yd player radius each, that's 2.5 yd center-to-center. The old sigmoid (mid=2.5 c-to-c) made a genuine 1.5 yd air gap score only ~40% catch probability — making the WR think it needed twice as much separation as real football requires. Redefined the geometry to edge-to-edge (eff_sep = center-to-center - 2×radius) and shifted the sigmoid to mid=0.5 yd on that gap. Result: 1.5 yd air gap → ~84% base catch probability, matching real football intuition. When the simulation's physics don't match the sport's geometry, every prompt that references "being open" is calibrated to the wrong standard.

**Parse error rate is not a prompt problem — it's a model reliability problem**
GPT enforces `response_format={"type": "json_object"}` which makes JSON output guaranteed. Ollama/qwen3:8b has no such mode; the integration omits the format constraint because earlier testing showed it caused empty `content` with reasoning only in `model_extra["reasoning"]`. Without enforcement, qwen3:8b produces prose 10–25% of the time. These failures are random in timing but consequential in effect: a parse error at the step before the critical throw (double_move t=1.0) caused the QB to hold one extra step, collapsing a 5 yd separation window to 0.58 yd PBU. The fix isn't a prompt — it's either model-level JSON enforcement, a stricter retry loop, or a different local model.

**WR facing computation: the model treats the coordinate system as a compass, not algebra**
After every route with qwen3:8b, the WR's facing direction during ball flight was wrong. Comeback: facing=71° instead of ~180° (caused a DROP). Zig: heading 180° (toward the CB) instead of 90° (away). Post_corner: facing=270° (due west) claimed to be "toward the QB at bearing 143°." The model computes bearing to the QB as a rough cardinal direction ("QB is behind me, so face 180°") rather than computing `atan2(dx, dy)` from actual position values. When the WR is at (5.47, 41.56) and the QB is at (26.65, 48.0), the correct bearing is ~143°, not 180°. The model has no ability to do this arithmetic mid-generation. Fix: inject the computed QB bearing as a number into the observation so the WR reads it rather than estimates it.

**The double_move was the only route the WR executed correctly — and it failed on QB latency alone**
On double_move, the WR correctly planned a three-phase route, held the rightward fake until CB rec=1, snapped back upfield, and called at peak 5.02 yd separation. The CB correctly bit the fake. The play failed because the QB produced a parse error at t=1.0 (the step before the throw), delayed one step, and the CB closed from 3.7 yd to 0.58 yd — PBU. This is the clearest example of a correct multi-agent play broken by a single point failure at the last moment. The WR did its job; the QB infrastructure didn't. When outcome measures like PBU vs CATCH are the signal, a one-step mechanical failure and a fundamental reasoning failure look identical.

**"Freedom" given to the WR collapsed route identity**
The WR system prompt gives the WR freedom to choose its own deception moves rather than prescribing a specific fake pattern. This was a deliberate design choice: we wanted to measure whether the model could generate genuine deception, not follow a script. The unintended consequence with qwen3:8b: when the WR is free to choose deception, it chooses the same thing on every route — a lateral fake followed by a break. "Go route" became "run a fake-cut near the line of scrimmage." "Slant" became "run upfield then oscillate." The route name and geometry were overridden by the model's dominant pattern. Freedom without route-shape constraints produces a single meta-strategy across all routes. This is the flip side of the GPT boilerplate problem: GPT locked into a verbal template ("jabbed left last step"); qwen3:8b locks into a behavioral template (fake-then-break). The solution is probably to inject the route's skeleton geometry as context ("this is a go route: no cut, maintain heading 0° for the full stem, call when open") without prescribing the deception choices within that skeleton.

**The angle blacklist is computed, not authored**
Previous attempts to break the 330°/30° jab habit used static prompt instructions: "vary your fakes," "don't repeat the same angle," "try a different sector." All failed — the model acknowledges the instruction and then does 330° again. The new approach computes the blacklist from play history at runtime: scan all prior steps, bucket headings into 10° groups, find buckets used 3+ times with CB ΔHdg <5°, and emit them as an explicit prohibition in the observation. The blacklist is play-specific, step-specific, and evidence-based. It doesn't ask the model to reason from failure — it makes the failure a named constraint the model reads as a rule. If this fails, it means the model is overriding explicit named prohibitions, which is a different (and more fundamental) problem than template habits.

**Route geometry in the pre-snap observation transferred to the plan, not the execution**
Round 9 injected explicit route geometry descriptions ("SLANT — stem upfield 2-3 steps, then cut sharp diagonally ACROSS the field at ~135°") into the WR's pre-snap observation, alongside a numbered phase schedule. The WR's pre-snap reasoning improved noticeably — plans cited the correct shape, described the correct cut angles, and mentioned the correct timing. Then the play started and the WR ignored all of it. Post_corner WR correctly noted "Phase 1: upfield, Phase 2: 45°, Phase 3: 315°" in its pre-snap plan, then jumped straight to 315° at t=0.1. The pre-snap context did not propagate forward. This is a direct consequence of stateless calls: each live step has no access to the pre-snap reasoning, only the current observation. The plan exists in the LLM's ephemeral context during the pre-snap call and is gone by t=0.1. The fix isn't a better plan — it's injecting plan-enforcement signals into every per-step observation.

**max_sep is a better diagnostic than outcome — and it revealed the WR never moves the CB**
After Round 9, the telemetry showed max_sep=5.02 on 8 of 10 routes, with the peak always occurring at t=0.0 (pre-snap). This is a more informative signal than win/loss: it means the WR is never creating separation, only inheriting it. The CB tracks with 10–20° heading adjustments every step without ever committing hips, so the WR's fakes register as noise rather than forcing a reaction. The outcome data (2C/3D/4PBU/1INT) measures who won; max_sep tells you whether the game was played. When max_sep equals the pre-snap gap, the route running effectively never happened.

**Removing editorial labels from the observation unlocked anticipation — the WR stopped calling at snap**
The WR observation had labels like "VERY OPEN — 5.0 yd body gap. CB is far away." These were intended as helpful summaries, but they did the opposite: the WR read "VERY OPEN" at t=0.0 (the pre-snap cushion) and immediately called for the ball, every route, every run. The model treated "VERY OPEN" as a sufficient condition to act, because it was stated as a conclusion rather than a fact. The fix: remove all outcome-oriented labels and replace with raw CB orientation data ("CB hips at 10°, 160° away from your position — advantageous angle"). Now the WR has to reason from the CB's physical state rather than read a verdict. After the change, the WR consistently held the stem for multiple seconds before calling. One label change produced the most visible behavioral shift of the entire project. The principle: anything stated as a conclusion in the observation will be treated as a decision, not a signal.

**"This is your window — explode now" is an instruction, not information**
The CB hip-turned alert read: "CB HIP-TURNED — N recovery steps left. CB cannot accelerate freely. THIS IS YOUR WINDOW — explode now." The explicit call to action ("THIS IS YOUR WINDOW") meant the WR immediately called and broke any time the CB entered cut recovery — even if the stem had barely started and the recovery was from the CB's own tracking adjustment. Replacing it with "CB is committed and cannot change direction freely yet" gives the same physical fact without prescribing the response. The agent then decides whether to act based on the full context, not because the observation told it to. Explicit call-to-action language in observations bypasses the reasoning step the agent should be doing.

**CB rec as trigger: any recovery fires it, not just earned recovery**
After fixing the pre-snap cushion problem, a subtler version emerged. The CB makes small heading adjustments every step to track the WR. Each adjustment that crosses the cut-recovery threshold produces a 1-step recovery penalty. The WR reads "CB rec=1" and treats this as "CB hips are committed the wrong way" — even if the CB was mid-tracking-correction and not committed in any meaningful direction. On 8 of 10 routes, the WR's stem was terminated 40-80% early because a minor CB tracking wobble fired the call trigger. The CB hadn't been pushed anywhere wrong — it was still facing the WR and capable of closing in the break direction. The model has no way to distinguish "CB rec from genuine hip commitment" vs "CB rec from a 1-step tracking correction." It requires spatial context the current prompt doesn't give it: did the CB's recovery follow from it heading *away* from my break direction?

**Reasoning is a better diagnostic than outcome — 4 of 7 catches were CB failure artifacts**
Round 11 scored 7C/3PBU. But reading the WR reasoning and CB parse error logs revealed that the "in" route's 7.7 yd separation was entirely caused by 6 consecutive CB parse errors that froze the CB while the WR accelerated away. The "double_move" CATCH happened because the QB threw so fast (0.11s ETA) that the CB couldn't close even though the double-move itself lasted one step. Outcome data measures who scored; reasoning logs measure whether the game was played correctly. The most important information about WR behavior was in the reasoning fields, not the resolution result — particularly the moments where the reasoning said one thing and the action did another (comeback: reasoning said "180° break," action was 270°; post_corner: reasoning said "315° terminal," action cut to 45°).

**Multi-phase routes reveal a consistent inversion: setup phase treated as catch phase**
On every route with 3+ phases (double_move, zig, post_corner, in), the WR called on the intermediate setup phase and never ran the terminal break. The post_corner is the clearest case: the route has a 45° post fake followed by a 315° corner break. The WR cut to 45°, called immediately, and the QB threw to that heading. The WR's own reasoning at t=0.4 said "Final cut to 45° will create separation" — it had renamed the fake as the final cut. The pre-snap reasoning correctly described all three phases ("stem, fake post at 45°, flip to 315°"), but by t=0.4 the WR had remapped the route so that 45° was the destination. This inversion was consistent: the model's training distribution likely associates "I make a cut → call for ball" as a tight pairing, so the first cut of any multi-phase route triggers the call regardless of what phase it is.

**The broken agent was hiding behind the more broken agent**
Round 11 scored 7C/3PBU with qwen3:8b; the CB was described as "working well." Round 12 with GPT gave 7C/1PBU/2INC and immediately surfaced a CB that never presses (5 yd cushion every snap), always picks swat intent (10/10 routes, even when it admits the arm tip is 3-5 yd from the ball path and can't reach), and self-induces cut recovery from its own minor tracking adjustments 2-4 times per route. The CB wasn't fixed between rounds — it was just irrelevant. When the WR calls at t=0.1 and runs the wrong heading, the CB wins without ever having to play real coverage. Fix the WR, and the CB's passivity becomes the binding constraint. Multi-agent systems have a dominant failure mode: the weakest agent absorbs attention, the second-weakest stays invisible until the first is fixed.

**Prompt-level phase gating works if it's structurally unavoidable**
The WR's multi-phase route problem (calling on intermediate phases, never running the terminal break) was addressed by adding a STEP 0 check at the top of CALL RULES: "Read YOUR HEADING NOW. If it says ~Xs remaining, then cut to Yd → you are in an intermediate phase. Do NOT call." This worked on 4/4 multi-phase routes (zig, in, double_move, post_corner called correctly on the terminal phase or held). The key design choice: placing it as STEP 0 — the first thing in CALL RULES, before any windows — makes it structurally prior to all other call logic. The same information buried lower in the prompt would have been overridden by the "Window A: call 0.1s before final cut" rule, which the WR had been applying to intermediate phases. Position in the prompt is load order for the LLM.

**Window A is unsafe on multi-phase routes — calling before the terminal break locks the wrong heading**
Window A says: "call 0.1-0.2s before your final cut so the QB throws as you break open." On post_corner, the WR correctly reasoned "final 315° cut in 0.2s, calling now to align with break." But calling locks the current heading — 45° (the intermediate post fake), not 315° (the terminal corner). The heading locked, the 315° break never executed, and the throw landed 18.8 yd from the WR. The WR was wide open (CB completely out of position) but got an INCOMPLETE anyway. Window A is only safe when the current heading IS the final break heading — i.e., there are no more cuts coming. On any route where the terminal break is still pending, Window A produces a heading-lock bug. The fix is to restrict Window A to phases where the observation says "this is your final break" with no upcoming cut.

**QB throws behind a WR running toward it — heading=180° misread as going upfield**
On the curl, the WR correctly called at heading=180° after a full 2.5s stem. The QB threw to (14.5, 68.4). The WR was already at y≈67.5 running in the -y direction (back toward the QB). The throw landed behind the WR. The QB reasoned "WR continues upfield then back toward QB" — it understood 180° conceptually as a "comeback" (eventually returning) but projected the WR's position as still going upfield before turning. The math: heading=180° means dy is negative immediately, so every step the WR moves to lower y. The QB computed a future y that was higher than the WR's current y, which is impossible on heading=180°. This is the same angle-to-direction inference failure documented earlier on the comeback route — 180° is semantically ambiguous (compass south, football "back to QB," math -y), and the model doesn't reliably pick the right frame. The fix is explicit disambiguation in the QB observation: "WR heading=180° means WR y-coordinate is DECREASING (running back toward you)."

**The CB doom loop: patience → drift → overcorrect → recovery → more drift**
The CB prompt told CB to "mirror" the WR and be "patient." Analysis of all 10 routes from Round 12 revealed a consistent failure loop: CB ran at heading 90° (lateral) during the stem while WR ran at 0° (upfield), so WR gained a yard per step. Lateral gap grew to 1-3 yd. CB then snapped 70-90° to close the gap — triggering cut_recovery=3. Speed collapsed for 3 steps. More ground lost. Repeat. By throw time, CB was 4-18 yd from the landing zone in all 10 routes, still picking swat. The root word was "mirror" — CB interpreted "mirror the WR's lateral position" as "run perpendicular toward their x-coordinate" rather than "run parallel at the same heading." One word drove the entire doom loop. The fix: replace "mirror" with the shadow model ("you are the WR's shadow — same direction, same speed, tiny x-tilts only") and specify that heading 90° and 180° are explicitly prohibited during the stem.

**Bearing vs heading: the CB misread a cut direction by 90°**
On the curl, WR breaks to heading=180° (back toward QB). At the step CB reacts, it reasons "WR made a real cut to 270°" — heading=270° is left/lateral, not downfield. CB had confused the bearing from its own position to the WR's new position (WR was to CB's left, so bearing≈270°) with the WR's actual new heading (180°). It corrected itself the next step. The mechanism: when a WR cuts, the observation gives both the WR's new heading and the geometric bearing from CB to WR — two different numbers, both expressed in degrees. Under the cognitive load of a cut reaction, the model occasionally reads the wrong one. This is the same family of angle-frame confusion documented earlier on the comeback and QB projection bugs. The fix isn't complete yet — a note like "match WR's HEADING, not the bearing from your position to WR" would help, but it requires explicit labeling in the observation so the right number is unmistakable.

**A hard distance threshold eliminated 10/10 impossible swat choices in one test**
The CB was picking swat on every route regardless of distance — including from 18.7 yd on post_corner, explicitly noting in its own reasoning that it couldn't reach. The prompt asked "can you realistically get your arm on the ball path?" and CB always answered yes. Replacing the qualitative question with a hard threshold (arm tip > 2 yd from ball path → play_man, no further evaluation) immediately produced correct behavior in the curl test: CB said "arm tip distance 2.5 yd > 2 yd, swat and interception physically impossible, only viable option is play_man." Same model, same context — the only change was making the rule structural rather than asking the model to derive it from physics. Qualitative "can you realistically" questions are not gates; they are prompts for the model to justify whatever it was going to do anyway.

**Reactive freedom: the CB needs burst available in all directions, not just the one it's running**
The new CB guidance adds a concept called "reactive freedom" — during the stem, the CB should run at a controlled pace so that cut_recovery=0 and burst is available in any direction. A CB that sprints toward the WR during the stem commits its hips in that direction, meaning when the WR cuts away, it pays full recovery cost from a poor hip position. The football analogy: a CB who crashes the WR before the cut has both less cushion and less ability to react. The prompt frames it as "the WR has freedom to go anywhere — maintain the same freedom to follow." Whether LLMs correctly apply a "maintain optionality" concept rather than always optimizing toward the current apparent target is one of the things this round will test.

**Shadow model validation at scale: the doom loop is gone, but geometry takes over**
After replacing the CB's "mirror" prompt with an explicit shadow model (heading ≈0° during stem, tiny x-tilts, flip on cut), the full 10-route suite showed CB using play_man on 8/10 routes and separation at throw time in the 2–4 yd range, down from 9+ yd when the doom loop was active. The change that mattered most wasn't the shadow model description — it was the hard distance gate (arm tip >2 yd → play_man, no further evaluation). In Round 12, the CB was declaring swat from 18 yd while acknowledging in its own reasoning that it couldn't reach. The gate made the decision structural rather than leaving it as a qualitative "can you realistically reach?" prompt the model always answered yes to. One structural rule fixed 8 of 10 routes in a single pass. The remaining two failures (go route at 1.84 yd, post_corner at 1.0 yd) are geometry problems, not decision problems — with the doom loop gone, the CB is actually close enough that the route geometry becomes the binding constraint.

**QB autonomy on the go route relapsed into premature throws — "wait for the call" is still load-bearing**
The anticipation doctrine (QB throws on its own read of a confirmed direction + projected separation, rather than waiting for the WR's call) was given a second chance with accuracy and direction-confirmation fixes. On the go route it relapsed into exactly the original failure: the QB threw at t=0.5s — its first legal step — a flat ~12-yard bullet into the CB's cushion. One run resolved CATCH, but only because the WR's jab fake happened to put the CB in cut_recovery; the ball still traveled <5 yards downfield, nothing like the over-the-shoulder deep ball a go route is won with. The lesson re-confirms the earlier "wait for the call was load-bearing" finding: with a weak model, an autonomous QB grabs the first cushion-window on every vertical, because pre-snap cushion *looks* like separation and the model has no reliable internal clock for when a deep route has actually developed. Decision for next session: revert to the call-gated QB, and go further — do not even invoke the QB agent until the WR has called. The QB's job shrinks to two things it can actually do well: place the ball, and decide *whether/when* to throw once a call exists.

**Distance ambiguity: "deep" relative to the WR vs. the QB's throw distance**
A subtle source of the premature go-route throw: every distance the designer reasons in ("10 yards deep", "beat them deep") is relative to the *WR's* downfield progress from his starting position. The QB observation, however, surfaces *throw distance* (QB-to-target). The model conflated the two — a 12-yard throw read as "deep" to the QB even though the WR had only covered ~5 yards from his snap spot. When a single word ("deep") maps to two different measured quantities in the same scene, the model will pick the one most readily available in its context. Observations and prompts need to state which frame a distance is in, and the WR-relative depth (current y minus snap y) should be the anchor for go-route timing, not the throw distance.

**Fix one agent, the next bottleneck appears: corner PBU is QB decision failure**
After the CB overhaul, the corner route produced a PBU at 1.27 yd separation. CB correctly played play_man. WR ran the route. The QB's own telemetry note read "contested (~1.4 yd projected at arrival); throw to (12.2,69.8) with lead away from CB." The QB knew it was a contested throw and forced it anyway — the sack clock was running, the route developed late, and it was the only window. This is a different category of failure: not a wrong model of the physics, not a bad execution of the route, but a risk-tolerance decision under pressure that produced a bad outcome. In multi-agent systems where agents are evaluated on shared outcomes, this kind of failure is invisible until the other agents are working well enough not to absorb the blame. Round 11's CB "worked well" because the WR was broken; Round 13's QB decision quality became the diagnostic only after both WR and CB improved.

**JSON enforcement with a system prompt instruction eliminated parse errors entirely for GPT**
Round 8 had 10–25% parse error rates with qwen3:8b (no JSON mode available via Ollama). Round 9 added a single paragraph to each agent's system prompt: "Respond ONLY with a valid JSON object. No text before or after the JSON." With GPT-5-nano, parse errors dropped to zero for WR and CB across all 10 routes. The only remaining failures were QB pass1 on slant (3 errors from token truncation, not malformed output — a different problem). The lesson is that JSON enforcement at the system-prompt level is sufficient for well-behaved models like GPT; the Ollama problem wasn't a prompt gap but a model reliability gap that prompt engineering alone can't close.

**The ball flew 3× too fast for the entire 2D phase — and nobody noticed because the agents adapted around it**
The 2D ball speed conversion used `MPH_TO_YDS = 1.46667`, which is actually mph→feet/s. Every throw in thirteen rounds of testing traveled three times faster than physics allows: a 20-yard throw "arrived" in ~0.3 seconds. The bug was invisible because the whole system calibrated itself around it — the QB prompt taught "the ball is in the air for 0.1–0.2s, the CB can close 0.5–1.0 yd during flight," the separation thresholds were tuned to a world where defense had no time to react to a throw, and the agents looked competent partly because the ball outran the consequences of their timing errors. The 3D rewrite derives flight time from the arc parabola, so the bug couldn't survive: a 30-yard bullet now hangs 1.3s, a loft 2.4s, and the CB closes the entire time. The lesson isn't "check your units" — it's that scaffolding tuned against broken physics becomes invisible debt. Every prompt number downstream of the bug was teaching the agents a physically impossible game, and all of it has to be re-learned in Round 14.

**For a fixed target, a throw has one degree of freedom — option labels must respect that or they're fake choices**
The original 3D plan defined arcs by peak-height ratio (bullet = 15% of distance, loft = 40%) and offered options like `bullet_fast` and `loft_slow`. But peak ratio fully determines flight time at a given distance, which fully determines ball speed — so the "speed tier" half of every label was a dead knob. Physics: once release point, target point, and catch height are fixed, choosing any one of launch angle, speed, or flight time determines the other two. The honest interface is to let the label map to the one real degree of freedom. We made arc = launch angle (bullet 15°, drive 25°, touch 35°, loft 45°), and the engine solves the parabola: flight time, required speed (validated against arm strength — bullets go out of range before lofts do), peak height. The options table the QB reads now contains no fake choices, and "throw over the underneath defender" is a geometric fact the table can state per arc ("clears CB in lane, z=5.4" vs "passes CB at z=1.8 — TIP/PICK RISK").

**First 3D contact: the QB chose the safest-sounding arc, not the time-optimal one**
The first live 3D play (GPT-5-nano, slant) ran clean — zero parse errors, all three agents reasoning about ball height unprompted. But the QB threw a *loft* on a quick slant: 2.4 seconds of hang time on a route whose entire premise is beating the CB's reaction window. The prompt explicitly says to prefer the flattest arc that clears the lane; the table showed bullet arriving in half the time. The CB used the hang time to close from 5 yards to 2.0 and the pass fell incomplete. One play is not data, but the shape of the failure is familiar from every prior round: given a menu, the model gravitates to the option whose name connotes safety ("loft" sounds gentle; "bullet" sounds risky) rather than computing which one the situation rewards. Arc choice is exactly the kind of spatial-temporal tradeoff this project exists to test — and the first sample suggests the label semantics may fight the physics. A second observation from the same play: with real flight times, the WR's ball-in-air phase became a genuine sub-game. The WR spent 2.4 seconds negotiating arrival timing — braking to avoid overshooting the landing spot, coasting, re-accelerating — reasoning that was structurally impossible when flights lasted two steps.

**Same play, same seed, opposite outcome — the loft was one sample, not a behavior**
Re-running the identical 3D slant (same code, same prompts, same seed 42) produced the opposite result: the QB picked *bullet* this time, reasoning "bullet arc minimizes CB time to close and stays in reach" — exactly the flattest-feasible-arc logic the prompt teaches — and the play ended in a CATCH at 3.94 yd separation. The seed controls the physics dice, but nothing controls the model. The previous play's "QB gravitates to safe-sounding arcs" hypothesis was built on one sample that didn't replicate. This sharpens a measurement problem that 13 rounds of single-run-per-route testing have been quietly carrying: a one-run round can't distinguish prompt effects from model variance, and any single-play conclusion about agent behavior is provisional until it repeats. The WR's flight-time negotiation, by contrast, did repeat — ETA arithmetic every step, accelerating when behind schedule, coasting twice to avoid overshooting the landing spot.

**Stateless agents re-litigate their conclusions: the CB detected the same cut five times**
During the 1.2-second flight, the CB's reasoning alternated on consecutive steps between "WR committed to a real cut to 45° — switch and close" and "WR still in stem, no real cut yet — maintain cushion." Its heading whipsawed (45° → 56° → 5° → 0° → 45° → 78°) and the oscillation triggered three separate cut-recovery penalties, costing any chance to contest the catch. The mechanism: each decision step is a fresh LLM call that re-derives "did the WR cut?" from the history table, and the derivation doesn't always land on the same answer. The WR's cut at t=2.0 was a fact by t=2.1 — but no agent has anywhere to keep facts. In 2D this barely mattered because flights lasted two steps; with real hang time the CB makes ten decisions per flight and pays for every flip-flop. Conclusions an agent has already reached need to live in the observation (e.g., the runner already stamps `detected_cut_t` in telemetry — the CB never sees it), or they get re-derived each step with variance.

**A viewer that compiles is not a viewer that renders**
The Ursina 3D viewer shipped "code-checked, not visually verified" — and the first human screenshot showed a white void with a sliver of field at the horizon. Two silent failures: ursina 8.x changed `color.rgb()` from 0–255 ints to 0–1 floats (every color clamped to white, no warning), and `camera.look_at()` simply didn't apply (the camera stared level at the horizon, seeing the ground plane edge-on). Neither raises an exception; both produce a window that opens, runs at 60fps, and is completely wrong. The fix loop only closed with a human in it: screenshot → diagnose → patch → screenshot. For GUI code, "imports cleanly and the API calls don't throw" verifies almost nothing — the render is the test, and only eyes can run it.

**QB lead: "throw ahead of the WR" needs numbers, not a direction**
The corner route was INCOMPLETE at the 3D baseline — not because of CB play, but because the ball landed short of where the WR ended up. The WR executes a 290° break and is in 3-step cut recovery at throw time: its speed is 2–4 yd/s and rebuilding. The QB projected constant-velocity arrival (same speed throughout), but the WR accelerated through that spot and was 1.4 yd further by arrival. The prompt said "lead the WR" but gave no numbers — the model understood the concept but applied near-zero lead (WR was still receiving the ball at their projected body center). Adding "0.3–0.5 yd ahead in movement direction + 0.3–0.5 yd opposite the CB" with concrete per-case examples flipped the corner from INCOMPLETE to CATCH sep=5.34 in one pass. The pattern: when a throw mechanic is described qualitatively, the model applies it symbolically. Quantifying "lead" as a spatial offset makes it executable.

**The hard arm-distance gate was fixing the wrong CB problem**
Round 13 added "arm tip > 2 yd from ball path → play_man, no further evaluation" to make the CB stop declaring swat from 18 yd. It worked — CB correctly chose play_man on 8/10 routes. But it created a secondary problem: the CB was now permanently in play_man mode because it almost always starts the ball-in-air phase more than 2 yd from the landing zone. A gate that eliminates impossible swats also eliminates possible sprints-to-the-ball. The correct model is a two-stage check: first, "can I race to the landing zone before the ball arrives?" (sprint_time < ball_ETA), then fall back to arm-reach if no. This gives the CB a realistic path to contesting throws it can actually reach, without the false swat problem. The lesson: a hard gate that solves one miscategorization will often create a mirror miscategorization in the opposite direction.

**Stateless agents pay a compound cost during long flights**
In 2D, flights lasted 1–2 steps; the CB had almost no time to make decisions. With real 3D physics, a 30-yd bullet hangs 1.3s and a loft 2.4s — the CB makes 13–24 decisions per flight. Every decision is a fresh LLM call that re-derives "did the WR cut?" from the history table. On the first 3D slant smoke test, the CB's conclusion alternated on consecutive steps: "real cut to 45°" → "still in stem" → "real cut to 45°." Each flip triggered a heading change, each heading change crossed the cut-recovery threshold, and the CB gave itself three separate recovery penalties trying to decide whether to react. It never contested the throw. The cost of stateless re-derivation scaled linearly with flight time — negligible in 2D, decisive in 3D. The fix (injecting the already-computed `detected_cut_t` into the CB observation) is only possible because the runner happened to be tracking it already. Without that signal, the only option would be to ask the CB to write its own persistent note — with all the scratchpad hallucination problems documented earlier.

**The fix-one-break-another loop was a sign the wrong layer owned the decision**
For months, every air-phase fix was a prompt edit to `wr_ball_in_air.txt`, and each one regressed a route the last one fixed — because prompts are global and the model is non-deterministic, so you can never tell a real regression from a reroll. The breakthrough wasn't a better prompt; it was moving the decision out of the prompt entirely. The WR's in-flight throttle (the brake-to-match-landing-timing math) was deleted from the prompt and replaced by a deterministic rule in the runner: run full speed to the ball, clamp against overshoot. The LLM kept its real freedom — *which direction* to chase a bad throw — and lost only the brittle physics knob it was bad at. The prompt got *shorter*. The principle that emerged: if a sub-decision is pure physics with a correct answer, the LLM should not own it; give the agent freedom over judgment calls and let the deterministic engine own the mechanics. A fix that lives in code can't regress from model variance, which is the only way to stop the whack-a-mole.

**One throw, two opposite failure modes — and fixing one summons the other**
The QB throws to a *fixed* landing point, but the WR is *accelerating* (often out of a cut). The instant the WR runs full speed, the system has two ways to fail and they pull in opposite directions. If the meeting-point solver under-projects the WR's travel (it was using instantaneous speed, which is low mid-cut-recovery), the ball lands short, the WR arrives early and freezes at the spot, and the defender closes during the dead time — a PBU. Fixing that by projecting the WR's full-speed ramp to top speed flipped the failure to the mirror image: the ramp over-projected (it can't know the WR is still hobbled by cut recovery, or that a double-move route will cut *again* after release), the ball landed beyond the WR's reach, and the result was INCOMPLETE — a strictly worse outcome. The solver and the WR's actual motion must use the *same* speed model or you just trade early-freeze for out-of-reach. The resolution: a cut-recovery-aware ramp scaled by a deliberate `safety < 1`, because the two errors are not symmetric in cost — under-projecting only costs a brief early arrival (still contested, often caught), while over-projecting is an automatic incompletion. When you can't predict a quantity exactly, bias the error toward the cheaper failure.

**Deterministic validation is the only way to tune against a non-deterministic agent**
The projection model couldn't be tuned by re-running the suite, because gpt-5-nano produces different throws and routes every run even at a fixed seed — a route flipping CATCH→PBU between runs says nothing about a code change. The model was instead calibrated *offline* against the recorded replay JSONs: for each route, pull the WR's speed and cut-recovery at the throw and the actual yards it covered during the flight, then compare to what the projection formula predicts. That comparison is fully deterministic and exposed the systematic over-prediction (every route, by 0.3–4.9 yd) and its two causes (ignored cut recovery; unforeseeable mid-flight second cuts) without a single new LLM call. The live suite was then just a confirmation, not the experiment. When the agent is noisy, move the measurement to the parts of the system that aren't.

**Telemetry that reports the peak instead of the decisive moment hides every regression**
The suite summary reported `max_separation` — the largest WR–CB gap at any point in the play. It clustered on identical values (5.02, 3.04) across totally different routes and had no relationship to whether the pass was caught, because the peak happens mid-route when the WR is at full stride and the CB hasn't closed, not at the throw or the catch. A PBU and a CATCH could show the same `max_separation`. Replacing it with `sep_at_throw → sep_at_catch` immediately told the real story — separation that *holds or grows* in flight (catch) versus separation that *collapses* (the defender closing). The metric you summarize on shapes which problems you can even see; a peak-over-time statistic is almost always the wrong one for judging an outcome that's decided at a single instant.

**The heading lock is a feature that becomes a footgun at the call step**
The runner mechanically locks the WR's heading from the moment it calls for the ball until the ball arrives. This is intentional: it stops the WR from drifting mid-flight and ensures the QB projects the WR trajectory correctly. But it created a non-obvious failure mode on Window A calls (calling 0.1-0.2s *before* the final cut): the WR is still running the stem heading (0° upfield), so it naturally outputs heading=0° in its JSON. That 0° gets locked. The WR then executes the cut to 45° or 180° one step later, but the runner ignores it — the heading is frozen. The QB has already projected a WR running upfield forever and throws there. The ball never finds the receiver. The fix was purely a prompt addition: "Your heading output on the call step is LOCKED for the rest of the play. Window A: output your BREAK heading — not current stem heading." Without the explicit instruction, the agent outputs what it's currently doing (stem=0°), not what it intends to do next (break=45°). The lock's transparency to the agent was the bug.

**Correct information in the observation doesn't guarantee correct behavior**
The curl overshoot illustrates this cleanly. At t=3.3 the WR is at the landing zone (y=61.5) with 0.48s until the ball arrives and a speed of 6.8 yd/s. The observation shows: current speed = 6.8, required speed = 0.2 (dist 0.1yd / ETA 0.48s), and an explicit instruction: "If required_speed < current_speed → BRAKE." The WR instead accelerated to 7.2 the next step and overshot to y=58.9 — 2.5yd past the catch point. Same model, same prompt, correct data injected — the agent read it and acted opposite to the instruction. This is a different failure mode from the heading-lock bug, where the agent genuinely lacked the relevant information. Here the agent had the information and ignored it. The distinction matters for diagnosis: information gaps are fixable with scaffolding; behavioral non-compliance suggests either the instruction is competing with a stronger prior ("keep running toward the ball") or the model is not actually computing the throttle decision from the observation at all. In this case, momentum toward the target may dominate — the model's default behavior is "run to the catch point," and once it's AT the catch point, there's no learned behavior for "you are already there, stop."

**Too much RNG was drowning the decision it was supposed to measure**
The catch resolution multiplied the entire catch probability by `catch_rating/99`, so a *wide-open* WR with a normal 80 rating completed only ~78% — a 22% drop on a perfectly executed, uncontested throw — while a 0.15 floor handed blanketed WRs free catches. Layer on a second contest roll, an occasional third gamble roll, and an uncommanded mid-flight "lane contest" die that no agent chose, and the outcome noise was loud enough to bury the very thing the sim exists to measure: the quality of the agents' reads. Replacing it with a three-zone model — open (sep≥1.8) deterministically completes, contact (sep≤1.0) deterministically fails to the CB, and only the genuinely contested 1.0–1.8yd band rolls a single attribute-weighted die — made good decisions reliably pay off and bad ones reliably fail. The lesson: in a sim built to evaluate an agent, RNG should live only where the situation is actually a coin flip. Everywhere else, noise is indistinguishable from (and drowns out) signal.

**Commitment and deception are in direct tension, and the prompt has to name both phases**
Letting the WR commit a multi-step plan (instead of re-deciding every 0.1s) fixed route incoherence — but it also killed deception: the WR ran sterile straight stems and the CB just sat on the break. Loosening the stem guidance to restore deception immediately overshot the other way: the WR broke at 2 yards on every route. The failure was that "deceive" and "run your route" read as contradictory instructions, and whichever was phrased more forcefully won outright. The fix was to make the prompt name two explicit phases with different rules — STEM (climb toward your cut depth; a fake is a jab that *returns* to the stem, never the break itself; do not call) vs FINAL BREAK (at the scheduled cut window, or earlier only on a genuine CB commit). Once a "fake" was defined as fake-and-return rather than just "deviate," the WR produced a real jab at 8 yards that committed the CB's hips, then broke off it — the behavior both instructions had been asking for separately.

**The model emitted the wrong heading *because* it correctly understood the physics**
On a curl the WR's own reasoning said "break to 180° (back toward the QB)," but the heading it emitted was 270° — and it then locked that and ran a sideline out instead of a curl. The cause was not confusion: the model knows turns are capped at 90°/step, so to reach a 180° reversal it emitted the first reachable 90° turn (270°) as "progress toward 180°." But the engine pivots a player to any commanded heading over multiple steps on its own — emitting the final 180° would have routed it correctly in two steps, whereas emitting the intermediate 270° (and calling on it, which freezes the heading) sent it to the sideline. A correct mental model of one mechanic (turn-rate limits) produced wrong output for another (the heading field wants intent, not a per-step-reachable value). The fix was one sentence: emit your FINAL break heading even if it's >90° from current; the engine handles the pivot. Worth remembering that a plausible-but-wrong output can come from the model being *right* about something adjacent.

**A tolerance band beat prose route descriptions for hitting cut depth**
For fifteen rounds the curl's route description told the WR to "hold the stem, then hook back at 180°" — and the WR kept breaking after ~2 yards. What finally got it to run the full ~12-yard stem was not better prose but a concrete CUT TARGET in the observation: "break at t≈2.5s, ~20yd downfield, within ±0.4s and ±2.5yd." A number with an explicit tolerance is checkable against the model's current state every step; a qualitative description ("a long stem") is not. The model could not tell whether it had stemmed "enough" from prose, but it could compare its current depth to a target with a band. The same grounding principle as the lead-distance and travel-time fixes: when behavior depends on a quantity, give the quantity, not an adjective.

**The lead hint structurally lies on a vertical — a pre-computed answer that's always wrong the same way**
The go-route premature throw survived a fix aimed at the wrong cause. The hypothesis was distance-frame confusion (the model reads "10 yards deep" as a 10-yard throw rather than the WR's downfield progress), so we surfaced the WR's depth-from-snap explicitly and labeled throw distance separately. It half-worked — at t=0.5 the QB held, quoting "1.7 yards depth" — and then it threw anyway one step later: INT, sep=0.96. Reconstructing the *actual* assembled prompt at the throw step (system + observation + step instruction, rebuilt from the replay) showed the real driver. The QB's LEAD HINT hands it a finished answer every step: "WR will be ≈(16.0, 59.8) — throw to the projected coord." That projection extrapolates the WR's current speed and the CB's current speed forward over the flight, so on a vertical it *always* shows the WR pulling deep and open — even at the moment the WR is still 2 yards *behind* the CB and hasn't beaten his man at all. The single fact that decides a go route — has the receiver overtaken the defender — was never in the observation, while a confident wrong projection was. The model wasn't being reckless; it was obeying a hint that's structurally biased toward "throw it deep now." A pre-computed projection isn't a neutral convenience: when its assumptions are systematically wrong for a situation (constant closing speed on a footrace that hasn't been won yet), it doesn't add noise, it adds a consistent lie the agent reasons faithfully from.

**Reading the assembled prompt — not the code, not the outputs — was the diagnostic that ended weeks of guessing**
Several rounds of go-route fixes (anticipation doctrine, break-visible gating, an asymmetry paragraph, the distance frame) each targeted a *theory* of why the QB threw early. The theories were inferred from the code and from the model's one-line reasoning strings. What actually located the bug was dumping the complete prompt the QB receives at the failing step and reading it as the model sees it — at which point two things were obvious that the code didn't make obvious: the LEAD HINT pre-answers the decision, and the *same* full observation (hint included) is fed to both pass 1 ("read the field, do you see a throw?") and pass 2 ("here are the arc options"), so the intended "form your own read, then see the math" split doesn't exist — pass 1 already contains the math. A scaffold accreted over many sessions drifts away from what its author thinks it says; the only way to know what an agent is actually being told is to render the final assembled string and read it, the way you'd read a diff before shipping it. The decision that followed was to stop patching and rewrite the QB prompting from that ground truth.

**Describing a timing constraint made the model rationalize around it; making it *emit* the constraint fixed it**
The WR broke its route absurdly early — cutting a 5-yard / ~1.0s slant stem at t=0.2 / ~0.6yd. The observation already stated the correct cut target ("break at t≈1.0s, ~5yd"), so this wasn't an information gap. Three escalating prose fixes failed in an instructive order. (1) Imperative language ("you MUST hold the stem") worked against the project's guide-don't-enforce principle and was reverted. (2) A factual consequence reframe ("break short and the CB is still on your hip") — still broke at t=0.3. (3) The arithmetic stated as fact ("your break is ~7 ticks away; a 4-step plan covers only 0.4s and can't reach it") *backfired*: the model used the new fact to **rationalize a shorter route**, reasoning "hold stem for ~0.3–0.4s, then break" — internally consistent around the wrong timing. Each prose fix gave the model something to narrate past. What finally worked was structural, not lexical: force every plan step to carry its **absolute timestamp** under a Pydantic structured-output schema (`chat.completions.parse`), so the model has to write `t=0.3` on its break step and confront that 0.3 ≠ 1.0. The WR then held the stem to t=0.9 and broke at the real cut window (CATCH, sep 4.35) — and did so by *choosing* to emit stem steps each re-plan, freedom intact. The lesson: when a model keeps reasoning past a constraint you've described, stop describing it and make the model **produce an artifact that encodes it**. A timestamp it must output is harder to argue with than a sentence it can read.

**Cloned per-step reasoning hid which step actually made the decision**
The plan mechanism stamped one plan-level `reasoning` string onto all of its queued steps, so the replay showed "maintain stem" on the very step whose action was the 45° break + call. For two sessions the early break looked like it had *no* break step at all — the diagnosis was masked by a logging shortcut, not by the model. The structured-output rewrite gave each step its own `t`-tagged reasoning as a side effect, and the per-tick narration ("t=0.7 eyes still on break point ~1.0s") immediately made the behavior legible. When an agent's decisions are batch-generated, per-step provenance in the log is not a nicety — without it you can be debugging a phantom.

**The deep ball is a fixed point the model can't solve in its head — so we reparametrized it**
After the lead-hint removal, the go route threw *late instead of early* but still failed: the QB lofted to a spot ~2 yards ahead of a receiver sprinting 8.8 yd/s, who then braked and waited 1.6s for the ball. The under-lead was 21 yards. The cause is a genuine circular dependency: where to aim depends on when the ball arrives (the receiver keeps running), but the arrival time depends on how far you throw (a deeper target is a longer throw is a longer hang is a deeper meeting point). No prompt wording resolves a fixed point — and asking gpt-5-nano (or any model) to iterate it mentally is hopeless. The dissolve is to stop choosing a coordinate and choose an *arrival time* instead. The receiver's locked path is a known ray in space-time, W(tau); for a given arc there is exactly one tau where flight_time(distance-to-W(tau)) = tau, because flight time grows sublinearly with distance while tau grows linearly, so the two cross once — a few steps of bisection. Compute that meeting point per arc and the QB just picks the arc, which now *is* the choice of how deep and how soon the ball meets him. The catch-22 was never the model's to solve; it was ours to reparametrize away.

**Which computations help, and which pre-answer the question under test**
Having just deleted the lead hint for pre-answering "throw here," the obvious next feature — show the defender's position and the separation *at each meeting point* — would have re-committed the identical sin one layer down: it hands back the open/not-open verdict, which is the entire skill being measured. The line we drew, and it's the reusable one: the engine may compute *landing physics* (where and when an arc intersects the receiver's path — pure kinematics with no judgment in it, and a thing the model genuinely cannot do mentally), but must not compute *openness* (whether the defender will be there). So pass 1 receives only raw positions, speeds, and history and must produce the open-window itself; pass 2 receives meeting points with no defender data at all. The test for whether a computed aid is legitimate is not "is it computed" — it's "does it pre-decide the thing we are trying to measure." A kinematic intersection is a tool; a contested-separation projection is the answer.

**A receiver will decelerate off his own go route to "arrive on time"**
With the deep throw finally landing on the receiver's line (29.6 yd, not 17.6), the play still died: PBU, sep 0.41. The receiver's ball-in-air controller computes required_speed = distance_to_landing / ETA and throttles to hit the marked spot exactly. On a curl, where he settles into a hole, that is correct. On a go it is self-defeating: the ball was led deep *precisely so he would run onto it at full sprint*, but seeing a fixed landing spot ahead he slowed from 9.0 to ~8.3 yd/s to avoid overshooting — surrendering the speed advantage the route exists to manufacture and letting the trailing corner stay glued. "Arrive exactly at the marked point" and "beat him deep" are opposite objectives; a single spot-arrival timing model cannot serve both, and applying the settle-route logic to a vertical throws away the catch.
