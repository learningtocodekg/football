# LLM QB Agent — What We Had to Add (and Why)

Notes for article. Tracks every scaffold addition made to get the model to behave like a real QB.

---

## Base Setup

- QB is an LLM (gpt-4o-mini / gpt-5-nano) called every 0.1s timestep
- Receives: current positions, speeds, headings of WR and CB, current separation, movement history
- Outputs: `hold` or `throw` with target coordinates and ball speed in mph
- WR runs a scripted route. CB plays scripted man coverage.
- No minimum hold time — QB was free to throw from snap

---

## Additions

**Hardcoded minimum hold time (0.5s)**
The QB was calling throw at t=0.0 before the WR had moved a single step — there was no history to reason from. Added a hard 0.5s gate before the LLM is even consulted. Without this, the model had no signal at all and just threw immediately.

**Route schedule in observation (cut time + heading + estimated cut position)**
The QB had no idea when or where the WR would cut. It was hallucinating target coordinates wildly — throwing to (36, 90) on a slant where the WR starts at x=16 and never gets past x=24. Added a per-timestep route schedule showing each cut's time, direction, and estimated WR position at cut time based on current speed/heading. Grounded the model's spatial reasoning significantly.

**Heading labels (plain English directions)**
The model was treating heading angles as raw numbers with no feel for field geometry. "Cut to 40°" meant nothing — it would interpret it as a huge rightward move. Added plain English labels: "diagonal upfield-right (toward right sideline)", "straight back toward QB", etc. alongside every angle.

**Removed projected throw options (pre-computed separation at arrival)**
The original observation pre-computed WR and CB positions for 5 time horizons and showed separation at arrival. This was meant to help but it backfired — the model saw "3.2 yd separation, feasible" and just threw without reasoning about whether the route had actually developed. Removing it forced the model to do its own reasoning from raw history.

**Ball travel time reference (bullet / regular / lob)**
The model had no concept of how long a throw takes to arrive. It would target a spot 1.3 seconds away and throw a "bullet" that arrived in 0.27s — completely mistimed. Added a per-timestep line showing actual flight time to the WR's current position at three speed tiers (bullet = max mph, regular = mid, lob = min). The model could now at least understand the magnitude of travel time.

**Two-pass decision system**
Single-shot decisions let the model commit to a throw without checking whether the WR would actually be at the landing spot. Added pass 1 (read the field — where do you think you want to throw?) and pass 2 (here are the concrete options at that target — will the WR actually be there?). Separates intent from commitment.

**WR projected position at arrival shown in pass 2 options**
Even with two passes, the model was targeting a future WR waypoint (e.g. "where WR will be at cut time") and treating it as a valid throw target right now. In pass 2, added a column showing where the WR will actually be at ball arrival for each speed option, plus whether it's catchable or a miss. The model now sees "MISS by 11.7yd" for every option when the route hasn't developed yet, and holds correctly.

**CB lockup duration (2 seconds before reaction delay kicks in)**
In replays the CB was giving up 4+ yards of separation within the first second. The CB was chasing a delayed WR signal from frame 0, so as the WR accelerated upfield the CB fell behind immediately. Added a 2-second lockup phase where the CB mirrors the WR's current position directly before the reaction delay engages. Now the CB stays tight for a realistic duration before the cut creates separation.

**PBU requires proximity (1 yard) or CB in passing lane**
A PBU was firing at 5.91 yards of separation — completely unrealistic. A cornerback 6 yards away cannot break up a pass. Changed resolution logic so a PBU is only possible if the CB is within 1 yard of the WR at arrival, or is physically in the passing lane (within 1.5 yards of the line between the landing spot and the WR).

**Pending-cut problem: pass 2 projections are wrong before the cut fires**
The two-pass system showed "MISS by 11yd" for every option while the route was developing — correct behavior. But the model would sometimes commit anyway at t=1.6s on a slant where the cut fires at t=2.0s. The pass 2 options use current WR heading/speed to project position at arrival. Pre-cut, those projections point the WR running straight upfield forever — so the landing spot is never near the projected WR. The model sees "MISS" and should hold, but occasionally reasons "the WR will be near there *after* the cut" and commits early. The fix is to make the pending-cut warning stronger in the pass 2 prompt: if a cut is imminent, the projections are explicitly labeled unreliable.

**CB agent: the model chose the wrong backpedal direction**
First run of the CB agent, the CB sprinted 22 yards the wrong way. The observation told the model "backpedaling means heading ~180°" and included an example — so when the CB was already *upfield* of the WR (between WR and end zone, which is the correct position), it read "backpedal = 180°" and ran *downfield through the WR* and kept going toward the QB. The CB went from 5 yd separation to 22 yd separation in 2.3 seconds. The fix wasn't physics — it was observation scaffolding. We replaced the abstract heading-vs-facing explanation with a **SITUATION block** that pre-computes the specific situation and emits a RECOMMENDED action with exact numbers: "WR is 4.2 yd downfield of you — backpedal upfield, heading ≈ 0°, facing ≈ 180°." The model follows prescriptions much more reliably than deriving the correct heading from a description of the coordinate system.

**CB agent: the model kept backpedaling after the WR blew past it**
Even after fixing direction, the CB would correctly backpedal while the WR approached, but then continue backpedaling *away* once the WR passed it. The situation flipped from "WR approaching" to "WR running away" but the model just kept doing the same thing. The SITUATION block needed to encode the phase transition explicitly: three named states — "WR approaching (backpedal)", "WR just passed (close gap)", "WR beaten by 2+ yd (CHASE)" — each with its own RECOMMENDED action and unambiguous instruction to flip hips and sprint. Without naming the phase change, the model doesn't detect it.

**ScriptedQB lead throw: naive projection vs. route simulation**
The first implementation of `ScriptedQB` used linear projection: take WR's current heading and speed, multiply by flight time, throw there. This broke badly the moment the WR was mid-cut — the WR's heading had just flipped to 40° and speed had shed from the cut, so the projection pointed way off field. The correct approach is obvious in retrospect: the WR's route is *fully deterministic* — we have `ScriptedWR.move()` and know the exact physics. The fix was to step the WR forward along its route using the same `move()` call the game loop uses, for exactly `eta` seconds. This is always accurate regardless of route phase. The lesson: when you have a scripted/deterministic system, simulate it — don't approximate it.

**Fuzzy landing zone: modeling human vision**
When the ball is in the air, a real DB doesn't know the exact landing spot immediately — they read trajectory and narrow in. We modeled this as ±4 yd uncertainty at release, tightening linearly to ±0.25 yd at arrival. The CB observation shows this shrinking zone each step. In practice the model latches onto the zone center from the first step anyway (treats it as point knowledge), so the fuzz is currently cosmetic. But it sets up future behavior where the CB must commit a direction before knowing the exact spot — which is the interesting decision.

**CB comeback route: the SITUATION block had a missing state**
The CB observation pre-computes a SITUATION block with a RECOMMENDED action. Originally three states, all keyed on `dy` (who is upfield of whom). On a slant or go route this works fine: CB backpedals while WR approaches, flips hips when WR passes. On a comeback, the WR runs upfield past the CB, then reverses to 180° and runs back toward the QB. After the cut, `dy` goes negative — CB is upfield of WR — and the old code emitted "you are between WR and end zone ✓ → backpedal." The CB dutifully backpedaled further upfield while the WR ran away in the opposite direction. Separation hit 14.17 yd at the catch. The fix was a fourth state: check `wr.heading` — if it's in 135–225° (running toward QB) AND CB is upfield, emit "WR has cut BACK toward QB — flip hips and CHASE downfield. Do NOT backpedal." After the fix: 0.1 yd separation, DROP. The lesson: the SITUATION block encodes field geometry logic that the LLM should not be trusted to derive. Every route type that can flip the positional relationship needs its own named state.

**Stateless calls mean the LLM can't detect phase transitions across steps**
Each timestep is a fresh two-message LLM call. The model has no memory of its previous reasoning or what it decided last step. It only sees the current observation plus a WR history table (last 10 steps of positions/headings embedded as text). This means phase transitions — "WR just cut, switch from backpedal to chase" — must be detected and expressed in the observation scaffolding, not left to the model to infer from the history. The history table contains the signal (heading changes), but the model won't reliably act on it without the SITUATION block calling it out explicitly with a named state and a RECOMMENDED action. Scaffolding is doing the perception work; the LLM is just executing the named response.

**Intent-aware facing during ball-in-air movement**
Once the ball is in the air the CB locks an intent (swat, go_for_pick, play_man). Early versions told the CB to always face the landing zone when the ball was in the air — correct for swat/INT, but wrong for play_man, where the CB should face the receiver (no facing requirement for play_man in resolution). The fix was to pass the locked intent into the movement observation and emit a FACING line that varies by intent: swat/INT → face the zone, play_man → face the WR and head toward the zone. This also captures the real football logic: a CB playing the receiver on a comeback should be in the receiver's face at the catch, not turning to look at the ball.

**WR agent: the model cut to the route heading immediately (1.8s early)**
The system prompt said "the cut time is a strict guideline (±0.2s)" but the live prompt said "if CB is giving cushion, consider cutting early to exploit it." The model resolved the contradiction the greedy way: it jumped to heading 40° at t=0.2 on a slant with cut_time=2.0. The route was telegraphed 1.8 seconds early. Fix: remove the early-cut escape hatch entirely, and replace it with phase-explicit instructions. The observation now shows which phase the WR is in with a hard directive ("KEEP HEADING NEAR 0°. Do NOT move to 40° yet"). Contradictions in the prompt are resolved by the model, not the prompt — always in the direction of least resistance.

**WR deception: "jab step" meant nothing without a mechanical definition**
The initial system prompt described deception as "false steps, speed changes, body fakes." The model's output: reasonable-sounding reasoning ("jabbing inside before the slant cut") with heading=40° every single step. It understood the concept but had no way to express it — there are no animations in a 2D simulation. A jab step IS a heading change. Fixing this required explaining exactly how the CB tracks the WR: it reads heading and speed, projects forward 0.5s, and moves to that point. Once the model understood this, it immediately started outputting heading=330° for one step then snapping back to 0° — correct behavior. The lesson: tell the model what the CB *computes*, not what football moves look like.

**WR deception: model understood "jab" but executed it for 20 steps**
After adding the mechanical definition, the model correctly jabbed left (330°) — but held that heading for the entire pre-cut phase (2 seconds). A jab that lasts 2 seconds is just running the wrong direction. The word "jab" implies brevity; the model interpreted it as a technique to apply, not a 1-step move to execute and release. Fix: show the exact step-by-step pattern explicitly ("step N: 330°, step N+1: 0°, step N+2: 0°, step N+3: jab again"). Also show the *wrong* pattern ("330°, 330°, 330° — this is NOT a jab, this is running left"). Making the failure mode explicit was as important as making the correct pattern explicit.

**call_t race condition: state mutation order matters**
The WR `decide()` call returned `call_for_ball: true` and the runner set `wr_agent.call_t = t` after the call returned. The *next* step's observation then read `wr_agent.call_t` to format "COMMITTED: called at t=X.Xs" — but `call_t` was still None because `decide()` hadn't been called yet for the new step. Fix: move `self.call_t = t` inside `decide()` at the moment of call validation, before returning. Observation reads state that was set during the *previous* decide() call — the runner setting state after the fact is always one step behind.

**CB "freedom" was fake: prescriptive scaffolding overrode the model**
The CB agent prompt claimed to give the CB free decision-making, but the SITUATION block was emitting `RECOMMENDED: backpedal, heading=0°, facing=180°` and `cb_pass1.txt` said "Copy the heading and facing numbers EXACTLY from the RECOMMENDED line. Do not compute your own." The CB was a lookup table with LLM overhead — it was never making a decision. The deeper problem: `_cb_situation()` had a geometry gap. WR jabs at 330° and 30° fall outside the `wr_going_lateral` threshold (45–135° or 225–315°), so they never triggered the lateral/intercept path — they always fell into the `elif dy < -0.5: backpedal` branch. Even if the CB had been free to decide, it was getting incorrect geometry analysis. The lesson: freedom in the prompt text is meaningless if the observation scaffolding has already pre-selected the answer.

**WR deception had no feedback loop**
The WR's history table previously showed only its own headings and speeds — no CB data except the static current-step snapshot. The WR could jab left all day with no way to know whether the CB was reacting at all. Deception without feedback is just randomness. Adding a side-by-side MOVE LOG (WR hdg/spd + CB hdg/mode/Δhdg per step) gives the WR the observability it needs: did my jab at t=1.2 actually change what the CB did at t=1.3? A Δhdg of 0° means the jab was invisible; a Δhdg of 15°+ means the CB reacted. The model can now close the loop.

## Observations

- The model's biggest failure mode is spatial: it has no grounded sense of where players are relative to each other or how fast they move across the field. Every scaffold addition was essentially providing that grounding.
- Pre-computing things "to help" often backfired (projected separation, min hold time from scenario YAML) because the model would anchor on the pre-computed number instead of reading the actual situation.
- The two-pass system revealed that the model's intent and its commitment were disconnected — it would say "I want to throw here" and then pick an option that physically couldn't reach the receiver.
- Travel time was a consistent blind spot. The model understood the concept when told, but couldn't apply it correctly without seeing explicit "WR will be at X when ball arrives" numbers.
- The two-pass system partially solved premature throws but introduced a subtler failure: the model reasons about *future* WR state (post-cut) while the projections show *current* heading extrapolated forward. It's doing the right qualitative reasoning ("WR will be open after cut") but committing before the cut validates that. The scaffold showed MISS correctly; the model overrode it with narrative reasoning.
- Tooling footgun (Ollama): `response_format={"type": "json_object"}` causes qwen3:8b via Ollama's OpenAI-compat API to return empty `content`. The thinking goes to `model_extra["reasoning"]` and the answer never makes it to `content`. Removing the format constraint and falling back to the reasoning field fixes it — but the symptom (silent empty string) is a bad failure mode that looked like a hang.
- qwen3:8b without `json_object` constraint: 73% parse error rate (16/22 calls) — model outputs prose essays in `content` instead of JSON. The rare successful calls were correct (held until t=2.2s post-cut, CATCH outcome), but the model isn't reliable enough for local use without a better JSON-forcing mechanism. Prompt engineering or a smaller/faster instruct model would help more than further API tweaks.

**Cut detection: a threshold gate isn't enough when the LLM jabs at the threshold**
The first cut-detection fix raised the gate to `t >= 0.5` to skip early jabs. In the next run, the WR jabbed at exactly t=0.5 — every route, every run. The model hadn't "learned" to time its jab to defeat the gate; it just always jabs early, and 0.5s is inside the early-jab window. A fixed time gate becomes fragile the moment the model has a consistent habit. The real fix is structural: require heading persistence (2+ consecutive steps) with a snap-back test. A jab by definition returns to 0° within one step; a real cut holds. The detector now uses a two-step candidate system — a heading change creates a candidate, which is either confirmed (held) or discarded (snapped back) on the next step.

**Richer deception coaching did not break the template habit**
After adding detailed deception coaching — hold fakes, speed fakes, double fakes, explicit anti-repetition rules — the WR still jabbed 330°/30° on every single route across all 10 runs. The habit appears to be at training-data depth, not context depth. Prompt instructions to "vary your fakes" or "don't use the same angle twice" are not strong enough to override a deeply baked pattern. What hasn't been tried: showing the WR its own action history so it can observe the repetition in context, or an explicit per-step callout ("you have jabbed 330° four times this play — the CB has read it"). The second is likely stronger because it makes the failure concrete and present rather than abstract and future.

**When the LLM misreads a heading, the error is systematic**
On the comeback route, the QB consistently threw upfield when the WR was running downfield (heading 180°). The LEAD HINT used angle notation that the model conflated with "running far upfield." The model doesn't compute in coordinate space; it maps angles to football concepts. Heading=180° plus a projected y higher than current produced a throw upfield — the model reasoned "comeback" ≈ "throw short to the sideline" without processing that y=63.7 is upfield of the WR's actual position at y=62.4. The fix was explicit English: "y is DECREASING toward QB." This bypasses the model's angle-to-direction inference entirely. Whenever an angle is semantically ambiguous in football terms (180° means "downfield" in coordinate space but "back toward QB" in football vocabulary), add a prose disambiguation — don't trust the model to derive it.

**The constraint "we guide, not enforce" determines which problems are fixable**
Several Round 2 problems (WR exits fake phase early, QB throws a lob on a short horizontal route, WR drifts sideways during ball-in-air) could each be fixed with a code-level rule. But the experiment is designed to measure LLM spatial decision-making. If we add a floor on fake phase duration, a speed requirement for horizontal routes, or a heading lock during ball-in-air, we're no longer measuring the LLM — we're measuring our own rules. These problems are left open as data: the model made a bad football decision, and that's what we're measuring. This constraint filters every observation and prompt change: the question is always "does this give the agent better information?" not "does this guarantee a better outcome?"

**WR calls for ball before executing the cut — the most common failure across all short-cut routes**
After four runs, the dominant single failure mode is the WR calling `call_for_ball=true` while still running straight upfield (heading=0°), well before the route cut should execute. On slant (cut at t≈2.0s, cut heading ≈315°), corner (cut at t≈2.5s, cut heading ≈290°), in (cut at t≈1.8s, cut heading ≈270°), and curl (cut at t≈1.8s, cut heading ≈180°), the WR called at t=0.5–1.0 while still at heading=0°. Each one resulted in a PBU, DROP, or INT — the QB threw immediately to where the WR was going (upfield), not where the route called for. The WR is applying one decision criterion ("I'm open by 2+ yards") as a sufficient condition for calling, when it's actually necessary-but-not-sufficient. The real condition is "I'm open AND my heading is approximately correct for the catch." The WR knows the catch heading — it's in the observation — but isn't applying it as a gating condition on the call. This is a case where the model's helpfulness instinct works against it: seeing separation, it wants to close the loop and call for the ball, without checking whether it's in the right position to catch it.

**Outcome variance across identical seeds: same route, different failure mode across runs**
The corner route gives a clear example of variance across runs without any code changes. Round 2: CATCH (WR executed the 290° break, reached 1.73 yd separation, abandoned it — O2). Round 3: DROP (WR abandoned break, regained separation via speed, called late). Round 4: PBU (WR called at t=0.5 heading=0°, never ran the corner cut at all). Three different failure modes from the same model, same seed, same scenario. The model's stochasticity produces a different "mistake archetype" each run. This means a single run is not a reliable diagnostic — a problem that appears fixed (O2 didn't manifest in Run 4) may just be hidden behind a different failure. Multiple runs are needed to distinguish "fixed" from "masked."

**MOVE LOG gave the WR visibility into CB reactions — the CB still didn't react**
The side-by-side MOVE LOG was added so the WR could see whether its jabs were moving the CB. The ΔHdg column shows how much the CB's heading changed after each WR move. In every route across every run, ΔHdg ≈ 0° on jabs. The CB simply isn't reacting to the WR's 330°/30° jabs at all — it stays in backpedal at heading 0° regardless. So the MOVE LOG is showing the WR exactly what's happening: your fakes aren't working. The WR's response: continue the same fakes. This suggests the failure is not observability (the WR can now see the feedback) but response to negative feedback — the model knows its fakes aren't working but doesn't switch strategies. The proposed fix (explicit per-step prohibition: "do NOT use 330° again this play") bypasses the need for the model to reason from failure toward strategy change, and instead makes the constraint structural.

**The call_tolerance gate existed in comments but never in code**
Four runs into A4, P-NEW emerged as the dominant failure: WR calling `call_for_ball=true` while still running 0° upfield, 1–1.5 seconds before the route cut. The state.md listed "call_tolerance gate in runner.py (±45° check vs cut_heading)" as a known mechanism. Auditing runner.py revealed it never existed — the comment described intended behavior that was never implemented. The call fired immediately whenever the LLM returned `call_for_ball: true`, with no heading validation at all. The lesson: a described mechanism is not an implemented mechanism. When a bug persists despite "existing" scaffolding, audit whether the scaffolding is actually in the code.

The obvious fix — add a 5-line heading gate that suppresses calls before the cut — was written and then reverted. The experiment constraint is that agents are guided via prompt information, never mechanically forced. Suppressing the call in code means the WR can never make the mistake, which means we can never measure whether the prompt guidance actually works. The approach instead: tell the WR in the prompt why calling early is self-defeating ("calling upfield means the QB throws upfield, not to your break point") and ask it to anticipate its future position along the route before deciding to call. If this fails, we learn something real about prompt limits. If a gate had been left in, we'd only learn that the gate worked.

**The WR applies "am I open now?" as a sufficient condition for calling — but the right condition is "will I be open at the catch?"**
The WR's pre-cut calls all share the same structure: current separation >2 yd, therefore call. The route cut is the future event that creates the catching geometry — but the WR is optimizing for present separation, not future catch position. At t=0.8 on a slant, a WR who is 2 yd from the CB while running straight upfield will NOT be 2 yd from the CB when the ball arrives at the slant catch point; the CB is running a closing trajectory. This is the anticipation failure your notes described: "the WR is basing everything on its separation right now. It is assuming the CB will keep this separation the whole time." The hard gate (heading must be within 45° of cut_heading) enforces the structural precondition, but the prompt change — explicitly asking the WR to project its own future position along the route and the CB's future position from its current trajectory — is the attempt to install the actual football reasoning. Whether the model applies this anticipation correctly without the hard gate is the thing to watch in Run 5.

**The persistent scratchpad: giving the WR memory across steps**
Each WR step is a stateless LLM call with no memory of prior reasoning. The WR was re-deciding to "fake left for 2-4 steps" every single step — it had been faking left for 3.7 seconds straight and still thought it was on step 1. The fix was a `wr_note` field in the JSON response: one line, ~120 chars, that the agent writes to itself and reads back next step via the observation. The key constraints: (1) overwrite it every step — it's a plan, not a log; (2) verify it against the move log — the note is not ground truth; (3) think forward, not just about right now. In practice, the WR still hallucinated ("2-step fake completed" after 1 step), but less severely. The mechanism is right; the grounding is incomplete. The next logical step is injecting the actual step count per heading from the simulation side ("you have been on 270° for N steps") so the WR can't miscount.

**The facing instruction/scoring mismatch: a bug hiding as a model failure**
The WR kept facing ~213° during ball-in-air when it should have been ~180°. The observation said "Bearing to landing zone: 36° — use this for FACING." But resolution.py scores facing based on angle to the *QB*, not the landing zone. On a slant, the landing zone is upfield of the WR (bearing ~36°) while the QB is behind (bearing ~185°). These are nearly opposite directions. The observation was telling the WR to do the wrong thing, and when it did the right thing (facing toward QB), the observation label called it wrong. The fix was to show "QB is at bearing X° from you — the ball is coming from that direction" and explain the concept rather than prescribe the angle. The lesson: when facing behavior looks like a model reasoning failure, check whether the observation itself is giving contradictory or wrong information first.

**Freedom philosophy: context replaces instructions**
The WR agent started with a highly prescriptive system — phase-by-phase directives ("CURRENT: stem phase, run 0°, HOLD this heading for the FULL phase duration"), pre-computed jab angle blacklists, turn cost tables. The redesign stripped all of this and replaced it with context: where the CB is, what the CB has been doing, what the CB's tracking mechanic computes, and how deception actually works mechanically. The agent is told the *physics* (CB projects WR forward 0.5s along current heading), not the *procedure* (jab left then go right). The philosophy: failures are data, not bugs. If the WR fakes wrong, that's a measured LLM decision under real context, not a missing rule. The tension: some failures (hallucinating step count, facing wrong direction) are not really "decisions" — they're information gaps the model can't reason through without scaffolding. The line between "give it context" and "give it a rule" isn't always obvious; the test is whether the scaffolding encodes geometry the model can't derive, or behavior we've decided it should have.

**Removing option labels unlocked genuine CB spatial reasoning — one change, immediate result**
The CB was picking "backpedal" every step because `_cb_situation()` emitted three named options ("backpedal / intercept / mirror") with pre-written tradeoffs, and `cb_pass1.txt`'s example JSON showed `"mode": "backpedal", "reasoning": "WR is still approaching and I want to keep cushion"`. The LLM anchored on the example and the first option. Across 10 routes and 5 runs, the CB's heading was 0°–10° on almost every stem-phase step. The fix was to remove all option labels and tradeoffs from `_cb_situation()` entirely — replace with a lean `GEOMETRY:` block (separation, bearing, WR projected position in 0.5s, intercept bearing) — and swap the example JSON to a forward-close movement. First run after the change: on slant_42, the CB varied its heading continuously (190°, 139°, 60°, 69°, 355°, 59°, 36°, 35°, 38°, 39°...), ran laterally at 36°–39° for five consecutive steps matching the WR's 40° break, and made a legitimate PBU at 1.24 yd separation. Prior run: pure backpedal every step, separation=4.83 yd, DROP. The mechanism: named options are prompts to pattern-match against, not prompts to reason from. "Backpedal" plus an example that shows backpedal means the model fills in backpedal. Raw geometry with no option names forces the model to actually read the data and decide.

**Static acceleration made the entire deception system fictional**
The physics engine had a single flat acceleration constant applied every step regardless of speed. A player at rest and a player at 7 yd/s both gained `accel * dt = 1.2 yd/s` per step. This meant: (1) there was no burst advantage from executing a plant-and-go correctly, (2) a WR who got their hips turned the wrong way could recover instantly, and (3) the CB could close at the same rate whether it had just committed to a hard cut or not. All the deception logic in the prompts — fakes, hip commits, stutter steps — was coaching agents on a mechanic that physically did not exist. You could write the best possible deception prompt and it would produce identical outcomes to a random heading, because the physics didn't differentiate them.

The fix: dynamic burst (`accel * (1 - speed/max_speed)`) plus a `cut_recovery` step counter. Any hard cut >35° at speed >3 yd/s triggers N steps of reduced acceleration — the player's hips are committed to the old direction and their weight is still shifting. A 90° cut at full speed costs ~4 steps of ~25% burst capacity. This is the mechanic that actually makes a hip-commit fake valuable: the WR plans the cut, so they have no recovery. The CB reacts to it, so they pay the penalty. The window is real now.

**The right level of abstraction for physics is "consequences", not "numbers"**
After implementing cut_recovery, the decision was how to surface it. Option A: show the raw number (`cut_recovery=3`). Option B: translate it into consequences (`RECOVERING — 3 steps left, burst ~43%`). Option C: an explicit alert when the opponent is in recovery (`"!! CB HIP-TURNED — 3 steps left. THIS IS YOUR WINDOW"`). All three are in the observation now, at different levels. The key insight is that agents will not derive consequences from numbers reliably — "cut_recovery=3" means nothing to a model that doesn't have a grounded sense of how 3 steps at reduced acceleration translates to yards of separation gained. The explicit alert ("THIS IS YOUR WINDOW") bypasses all that inference and gives the model a named decision point. Same information; the only question is whether the model reads it correctly or acts on it correctly. Named alerts are acted on; numbers are noted.

**The scratchpad feedback loop: hallucinated history becomes injected context**
The WR has a one-line `wr_note` scratchpad — it writes a note to itself each step, and the next step's observation injects it as "YOUR NOTE (from last step)." At t=0.0, the move log is empty (no actions have occurred) and the note field reads "(none yet)." Despite this, the LLM outputs `wr_note = "jabbed left last step so returning to 0 now"` on the very first step. That hallucinated note is then injected into t=0.1 as fact. The LLM reads its own invented history, confirms it, and the loop runs for the entire play — the boilerplate string appeared verbatim on 6 of 10 routes, including mid-fake on the zig (where it aborted a real 3-step fake at t=0.9 by claiming to be "returning to 0"). The fix is a grounding rule: the note must be consistent with what the move log shows, and if the log is empty, the note must be forward-looking. But the deeper issue is that anything injected into the prompt as "what you did" will be treated as fact by the model, even if the model wrote it itself one step ago as a confabulation. Scratchpad memory and ground truth are not the same thing, and the observation needs to make that distinction explicit every step.

**The CB backpedal problem was ideological, not mechanical**
After the physics overhaul, the CB observation included burst status, cut_recovery columns in history, explicit "WR HIP-TURNED — CLOSE NOW" alerts, and raw bearing geometry. The CB still spent 15–25 consecutive steps per route with near-identical reasoning ("WR is still approaching, keeping cushion"), including steps after the WR had already completed its cut and was running away. The issue wasn't missing data — the CB had everything it needed. The issue was the ideology: `cb_system.txt` said "patience beats reactivity" and "do not match heading changes immediately," and provided no failure anchor. The CB had no model of what losing looked like. It didn't know that backpedaling straight upfield while the WR drifts sideways is already a loss, or that continuing to backpedal after a confirmed cut means every step costs a yard it can't recover. Without a concrete danger model ("3+ yd separation on break heading + ball in air = catch"), the patience doctrine applied to everything — even situations where immediate reaction was obviously required. Correct behavior and safety behavior coincided on most steps, which reinforced the template. The CB only escaped it when a ball-in-air intent switch fired.

**A 3-step code gate was categorized as a prompt/behavioral problem**
Round 7's problems.md listed "S3 — CB frozen for 3 steps at pre-snap (HIGH, ALL ROUTES)" and the hypothesis was "make the physics prompting more prominent." The root cause was a single line in runner.py: `if cb_agent is not None and "CB1" in states and t >= 0.3:`. The CB agent literally wasn't called for t=0.0, 0.1, 0.2 — it wasn't making bad decisions, it wasn't being called at all. The free WR release wasn't a reasoning failure; it was a hardcoded gate. When a behavioral symptom appears consistently across all routes and all runs, check for code-level causes before assuming prompt causes. A behavior that is perfectly consistent across all runs is more likely structural than stochastic.

**_build_options assumed constant WR speed, making post-cut throws systematically wrong**
The QB's pass-2 option table showed "WR will be at (X, Y) when ball arrives" for each throw speed, using `wr_speed * eta` to project position. This assumed the WR's current speed would hold for the entire flight. On post-cut routes, the WR is in cut_recovery at call time — speed is 2–6 yd/s and rebuilding. The QB would throw to (20.3, 63.5) because the projection showed the WR there, but the WR would accelerate through that spot and be at 64.3 by arrival. The QB was computing the right lead for a WR running at constant 2 yd/s; the WR was running at 7 yd/s by the time the ball got there. The fix adds a two-row projection per option when cut_recovery > 0: one using current speed (conservative, where the WR will be if recovery is slow) and one using rebuilt max speed (optimistic, where the WR will be if they accelerate normally). This gives the QB a range and flags the uncertainty, rather than presenting a single incorrect projection as fact.

**cut_recovery was working in physics but absent from every replay**
The physics engine correctly computed cut_recovery after the overhaul — speed drops were clean and proportional to cut angle. But the `_player_snap()` function that builds each step's replay JSON simply never included `cut_recovery` in the output dict. Agents were referencing "rec" values in their reasoning, but those values couldn't be verified from replays because the field wasn't there. The physics were real; the observability wasn't. This is a different failure mode from agents ignoring available data — the data existed in the running simulation but never reached the persistent record. When validating a new physics feature, check that the feature's state is actually written to replay output, not just that the physics code runs correctly.

**Switching models resets all behavioral progress — new failure modes replace old ones**
Moving from gpt-5-nano to qwen3:8b via Ollama produced an entirely different failure profile. The boilerplate "jabbed left last step" hallucination (which ran for 7 rounds with GPT) disappeared — replaced by route shape confusion: the WR executed a generic fake-then-break template on 6 of 10 routes, ignoring the route geometry entirely. "Go" became a fake-cut near the line of scrimmage. "Slant" became a seam. "Curl" became a 9-step lateral drift. The model had learned none of the route-specific context from prior prompt work — it needed to re-learn it with its own training distribution. Behavioral calibration is model-specific, not prompt-specific.

**The model generated the old API schema even after the schema changed**
The QB pass-2 prompt specifies `{"action": "throw", "option": "<label>", "reasoning": "..."}` — the model picks a speed tier by label (bullet/hard/medium/soft/lob) and the runner resolves coordinates from the label. Three consecutive throw attempts in one run produced parse errors with this raw: `{"action":"throw","target_coord":[17.5,53.5],"ball_speed_mph":45,"reasoning":"Lead WR...`. The model was generating the *previous* schema — direct coordinates and speed — instead of the current label format. `response_format={"type":"json_object"}` guarantees valid JSON, not correct field names. The model's training distribution included the old format; the current prompt description wasn't strong enough to override it. Fix: add a fallback parser that accepts both formats. The diagnostic was only possible because we added full raw logging — the original 120-char truncation showed a syntactically incomplete string that looked like a different error entirely.

**Call timing is asymmetric: the window closes before it looks open**
The WR consistently called for the ball 1–2 steps after CB recovery had started, not at the start. At t=2.7, after observing "CB rec=3 and 2 yd gap exists", the WR wrote: "increase speed to force separation before CB recovers" — then called at t=2.8. With CB 3-step recovery starting at t=2.6, the throw at t=2.9 arrived at t=3.2 after the window had fully closed. The model knew it had a window but treated it as time to gain *more* separation, not as the throw window itself. The fix that worked: a "CALL TIMING" note explaining the math explicitly ("QB takes 1 step to process, ball takes 0.2-0.5s to fly — call at rec=3, ball arrives at rec≈0; call at rec=1, ball arrives after recovery"). Also critical: "CB rec > 0 IS clear separation — you do not need to see the gap grow visually first." Without that second sentence, the model acknowledged the window and waited for visual confirmation that it had grown. By then it was gone.

**Separation in real football is 1.5 yards, not 3**
The original system used 3.0 yd center-to-center as "open." Real NFL coverage is considered broken at ~1.5 yd edge-to-edge air gap between bodies. With 0.5 yd player radius each, that's 2.5 yd center-to-center. The old sigmoid (mid=2.5 c-to-c) made a genuine 1.5 yd air gap score only ~40% catch probability — making the WR think it needed twice as much separation as real football requires. Redefined the geometry to edge-to-edge (eff_sep = center-to-center - 2×radius) and shifted the sigmoid to mid=0.5 yd on that gap. Result: 1.5 yd air gap → ~84% base catch probability, matching real football intuition. When the simulation's physics don't match the sport's geometry, every prompt that references "being open" is calibrated to the wrong standard.

**Parse error rate is not a prompt problem — it's a model reliability problem**
GPT enforces `response_format={"type": "json_object"}` which makes JSON output guaranteed. Ollama/qwen3:8b has no such mode; the integration omits the format constraint because earlier testing showed it caused empty `content` with reasoning only in `model_extra["reasoning"]`. Without enforcement, qwen3:8b produces prose 10–25% of the time. These failures are random in timing but consequential in effect: a parse error at the step before the critical throw (double_move t=1.0) caused the QB to hold one extra step, collapsing a 5 yd separation window to 0.58 yd PBU. The fix isn't a prompt — it's either model-level JSON enforcement, a stricter retry loop, or a different local model.

**WR facing computation: the model treats the coordinate system as a compass, not algebra**
After every route with qwen3:8b, the WR's facing direction during ball flight was wrong. Comeback: facing=71° instead of ~180° (caused a DROP). Zig: heading 180° (toward the CB) instead of 90° (away). Post_corner: facing=270° (due west) claimed to be "toward the QB at bearing 143°." The model computes bearing to the QB as a rough cardinal direction ("QB is behind me, so face 180°") rather than computing `atan2(dx, dy)` from actual position values. When the WR is at (5.47, 41.56) and the QB is at (26.65, 48.0), the correct bearing is ~143°, not 180°. The model has no ability to do this arithmetic mid-generation. Fix: inject the computed QB bearing as a number into the observation so the WR reads it rather than estimates it.

**The double_move was the only route the WR executed correctly — and it failed on QB latency alone**
On double_move, the WR correctly planned a three-phase route, held the rightward fake until CB rec=1, snapped back upfield, and called at peak 5.02 yd separation. The CB correctly bit the fake. The play failed because the QB produced a parse error at t=1.0 (the step before the throw), delayed one step, and the CB closed from 3.7 yd to 0.58 yd — PBU. This is the clearest example of a correct multi-agent play broken by a single point failure at the last moment. The WR did its job; the QB infrastructure didn't. When outcome measures like PBU vs CATCH are the signal, a one-step mechanical failure and a fundamental reasoning failure look identical.

**"Freedom" given to the WR collapsed route identity**
The WR system prompt gives the WR freedom to choose its own deception moves rather than prescribing a specific fake pattern. This was a deliberate design choice: we wanted to measure whether the model could generate genuine deception, not follow a script. The unintended consequence with qwen3:8b: when the WR is free to choose deception, it chooses the same thing on every route — a lateral fake followed by a break. "Go route" became "run a fake-cut near the line of scrimmage." "Slant" became "run upfield then oscillate." The route name and geometry were overridden by the model's dominant pattern. Freedom without route-shape constraints produces a single meta-strategy across all routes. This is the flip side of the GPT boilerplate problem: GPT locked into a verbal template ("jabbed left last step"); qwen3:8b locks into a behavioral template (fake-then-break). The solution is probably to inject the route's skeleton geometry as context ("this is a go route: no cut, maintain heading 0° for the full stem, call when open") without prescribing the deception choices within that skeleton.

**The angle blacklist is computed, not authored**
Previous attempts to break the 330°/30° jab habit used static prompt instructions: "vary your fakes," "don't repeat the same angle," "try a different sector." All failed — the model acknowledges the instruction and then does 330° again. The new approach computes the blacklist from play history at runtime: scan all prior steps, bucket headings into 10° groups, find buckets used 3+ times with CB ΔHdg <5°, and emit them as an explicit prohibition in the observation. The blacklist is play-specific, step-specific, and evidence-based. It doesn't ask the model to reason from failure — it makes the failure a named constraint the model reads as a rule. If this fails, it means the model is overriding explicit named prohibitions, which is a different (and more fundamental) problem than template habits.

**Route geometry in the pre-snap observation transferred to the plan, not the execution**
Round 9 injected explicit route geometry descriptions ("SLANT — stem upfield 2-3 steps, then cut sharp diagonally ACROSS the field at ~135°") into the WR's pre-snap observation, alongside a numbered phase schedule. The WR's pre-snap reasoning improved noticeably — plans cited the correct shape, described the correct cut angles, and mentioned the correct timing. Then the play started and the WR ignored all of it. Post_corner WR correctly noted "Phase 1: upfield, Phase 2: 45°, Phase 3: 315°" in its pre-snap plan, then jumped straight to 315° at t=0.1. The pre-snap context did not propagate forward. This is a direct consequence of stateless calls: each live step has no access to the pre-snap reasoning, only the current observation. The plan exists in the LLM's ephemeral context during the pre-snap call and is gone by t=0.1. The fix isn't a better plan — it's injecting plan-enforcement signals into every per-step observation.

**max_sep is a better diagnostic than outcome — and it revealed the WR never moves the CB**
After Round 9, the telemetry showed max_sep=5.02 on 8 of 10 routes, with the peak always occurring at t=0.0 (pre-snap). This is a more informative signal than win/loss: it means the WR is never creating separation, only inheriting it. The CB tracks with 10–20° heading adjustments every step without ever committing hips, so the WR's fakes register as noise rather than forcing a reaction. The outcome data (2C/3D/4PBU/1INT) measures who won; max_sep tells you whether the game was played. When max_sep equals the pre-snap gap, the route running effectively never happened.

**JSON enforcement with a system prompt instruction eliminated parse errors entirely for GPT**
Round 8 had 10–25% parse error rates with qwen3:8b (no JSON mode available via Ollama). Round 9 added a single paragraph to each agent's system prompt: "Respond ONLY with a valid JSON object. No text before or after the JSON." With GPT-5-nano, parse errors dropped to zero for WR and CB across all 10 routes. The only remaining failures were QB pass1 on slant (3 errors from token truncation, not malformed output — a different problem). The lesson is that JSON enforcement at the system-prompt level is sufficient for well-behaved models like GPT; the Ollama problem wasn't a prompt gap but a model reliability gap that prompt engineering alone can't close.
